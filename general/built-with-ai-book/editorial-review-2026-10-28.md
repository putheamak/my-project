# Editorial Review: *Built with AI, No Coding*, by Mak Puthea

Independent editor and fact-checker review · 28 October 2026 · Draft reviewed: `book-full.md` (about 26,000 words) and `launch-kit-draft.md`

---

## 1. Overall verdict

**Score now: 6.5 / 10.** The book is honest, clear and unusual in a crowded market. But it takes too long to reach the building, it repeats its best stories, and its results are still placeholders.

**Score if the top fixes are made: 8 / 10.** To get there: shorten Part One, fill the ⭐ result gaps, tell each anecdote once, correct the handful of statistics below, and soften the passages about the former employer.

The core idea is strong: a non-programmer in Phnom Penh keeps an honest build journal with real costs and real failures, including 2,546 views and zero bookings, and zero affiliate sales. That is a real point of difference from the "AI + KDP = ATM" books. The weak point is the reader's first impression. A buyer who opens "Look Inside" reads about corporate politics, an AI-safety resignation, IMF tables and universal basic income. Nothing is built until roughly 27% of the way through the book.

Most of the Chapter 2–3 statistics are correct and well sourced. Four need adjusting: the Microsoft country figures (outdated), the WEF jobs figures (presented as if AI alone causes them), the Oxford data-centre wording, and "British" for Jacob Coxon (not verified). Two more have weak or second-hand sources: the ITU "96%" and the garment-worker note. I found no invented facts.

## 4. Top 10 issues, ranked by impact

**1. The reader waits too long for the building (structure and "Look Inside").**
The Introduction and Part One take about 6,700 of roughly 25,000 words (about 27%). Chapters 2 and 3 alone are about 3,200 words of AI futures, geopolitics and UBI. Amazon's "Look Inside" shows roughly the first 10%: the reader sees the copyright page, the introduction, office politics and the start of an AI-safety story, and not one tool.
*Fix:* Merge Chapters 2 and 3 into one chapter of about 1,500–1,800 words, called "Why This Matters, Especially Here". Keep the Coxon opening, the six gaps (one or two sentences each), Cambodia's ladder and your personal belief. Cut or move to a short closing essay: "Three possible futures", "A world already under pressure", "If superintelligence comes and there are not enough jobs" and "Two possible endings". Cut Chapter 1's bubble and Keynes passage by about a third. Then open the Introduction with a scene: the booking appearing in the office group (Ch 8, "The moment it worked") or the Command Prompt numbers matching SPSS.

**2. Chapters 2–3 read like a policy report, not like you.**
Your voice disappears there, and the material dates quickly (Middle East paragraph, Microsoft quarterly figures). It also strays from the title's promise of tools, costs and mistakes.
*Fix:* For each statistic you keep, add one sentence of what *you* saw in Phnom Penh. Make the corrections in section 2. Remove every claim that will look stale by mid-2027.

**3. The results are still placeholders, so the title's promise is only half-kept.**
There are ⭐ gaps in Ch 9 (post and sales numbers), Ch 10 (videos made), Ch 11 (launch) and Ch 12 (hours). The booking app has no usage data at all. A Western buyer of "Real Business Tools, Real Costs and Real Mistakes" will expect *some* outcomes.
*Fix:* Before the December upload, add a short "Where things stand: [date]" box at the end of Chapters 8–11 with real numbers: bookings received through the mini app, the share of sellers using it, blog visitors, clicks and sales, videos posted, hours spent. Zeros are fine and on-brand. Missing numbers are not.

**4. The same anecdotes are told two or three times.**
- Emoji as empty boxes: Ch 6, Ch 10, Ch 12.
- Wrong phone digit on the poster: Ch 6, Ch 10.
- Unconfirmed same-day COD promise: Ch 6, Ch 10, Ch 12.
- Pinterest and the false country: Ch 6, Ch 9, Ch 12.
- 2,546 views and zero bookings: Ch 6, Ch 10.
- Travel guide demand check: Ch 4, Ch 6, Ch 11.
- Typing "go": Ch 6, Ch 9.
- The booking app described in full: Ch 4 and Ch 8.

*Fix:* Tell each story fully once, in the project chapter where it happened. In Ch 6 and Ch 12, use one sentence and a cross-reference ("as Chapter 10 describes"). Cut Ch 4's project descriptions to 2–3 sentences each.

**5. Defamation and identification risk around the former employer.**
The company is unnamed, but it is described as "a leading consumer brand", "one of the largest companies in the country" (Ch 3), with your title, three years' tenure and your leaving date. Locals will identify it. Lines such as "New people were hired under conditions that, in my view, raised serious questions of ethics and professionalism" and "Some colleagues were pushed out" are allegations about identifiable people. Cambodia has criminal defamation provisions.
*Fix:* Delete the "ethics and professionalism" sentence. Change "pushed out" to "Some colleagues left". Remove "one of the largest companies in the country" from Ch 3. Present the politics as your experience and feeling, not as facts about named roles. Keep the career-risk lesson, which is the valuable part.

**6. The "voice" sounds AI-polished in places and is very uniform.**
The Introduction and Part Two use a formal, British, polished register ("whereas", "precisely", "genuinely", "lengthy deliberation", "a matter of respect", "It was the least exciting answer I could have received, and precisely the one I needed."). Almost every section ends with a bold one-line maxim, and Ch 6 adds eight "**Why it matters:**" blocks. Readers of AI books are alert to this pattern, and some reviewers will say "this reads like ChatGPT".
*Fix:* Keep about a third of the bold maxims. Drop "Why it matters" labels (fold them into prose). In each chapter, restore 3–5 of your own short sentences and phrasings (like "I was really surprised" and "very small demand", which are excellent). Allow a little imperfect, direct English: it is your differentiator.

**7. The "how to build" promise is thinner than the description implies.**
Ch 4 says "In Part Two, you will learn how to build your own version of each". The Builder's Notes for the booking app, blog engine and video factory assume steps a beginner cannot do alone: where Claude Code runs, installing it, a terminal, Node.js, ffmpeg or a headless browser for the video factory, GitHub secrets, and the build step before "paste the single file" (your own project uses `node build.mjs`).
*Fix:* Add a 1–2 page "Getting set up" section in Ch 5: how to open Claude Code (desktop, web or terminal), what a repository is, and how to let the AI install things. Add a line to each Builder's Note: "If you are a complete beginner, start by pasting this note into Claude Code and ask it to walk you through each step". Soften Ch 4 to "you will see how each was built, with starter prompts to build your own".

**8. Notes, formatting and Kindle readiness.**
There are three separate sections all called "Notes", each restarting at 1. Part Three's notes sit after "A Request to the Reader", and the notes are missing from the Contents. Unicode superscripts (¹ ²) will not be tappable on Kindle. ⭐ placeholders and `<!-- -->` checklists must be removed.
*Fix:* Put one "Notes" section at the end, grouped by chapter ("Chapter 2", "Chapter 3"...), with numbering restarting per chapter and linked superscripts (Kindle Create or Vellum/Atticus handle this). Add "Notes" to the Contents. Do a search for "⭐" and "<!--" before export.

**9. Cost claim needs one more caveat.**
"The tools behind all four projects cost me about $31 a month" is stated in the Introduction and description as fact, but $10 of it is an estimate (checklist item 1). Claude Pro also has usage limits that heavy Claude Code users can hit, and some countries add VAT/GST to digital subscriptions.
*Fix:* Replace the estimate with your real API bills before publishing. Add one sentence in Ch 5: "Pro has usage limits; on very busy days I had to wait for them to reset" (if true), and "prices exclude local tax".

**10. Privacy: personal contact details in a global book.**
A personal Gmail and personal phone number printed in a book sold worldwide will attract spam and scam calls. This is also exactly the "doors" risk your Ch 12 warns about.
*Fix:* Create a book-only email (for example, builtwithai.book@...) and a Telegram channel or link. Drop the personal phone number.

Consider one line acknowledging that his announcement was also criticised (allegations that a PR firm coordinated it, disputes over his old posts), or noting that Anthropic's alignment lead publicly agreed with him (check that before using it). Either keeps you even-handed. The "What it could mean for daily life" section is a list of predictions and could be halved.

- **Ch 9.** The most marketable chapter (the passive-income truth). Fix the Amazon image and price rule wording. - **Ch 11.** The origin story (AI + KDP = ATM) is excellent and very marketable. The MLK passage and the "my working life has known its share of highs and lows" paragraph feel inserted and generic. Shorten them to two sentences in your own words. Fix the "never invents" contradiction.

- **Glossary.** Useful and well pitched.
- **About the Author.** Good, apart from the contact details (Issue 10). "No online profile was found" for the author is a market weakness: create a simple author page (Amazon Author Central plus one web page) before launch.

## 7. Launch kit feedback

**Title and subtitle.** The main title is good: short, clear and searchable. The subtitle is long (about 95 characters) but carries strong keywords. One risk: "Real Business Tools" plus "Real Mistakes" invites the question "real results?". Make sure the description admits early-stage results (it already does: "including those that have yet to arrive"). Optional improvement: "...Real Costs and Honest Results from a Small-Business Owner in Cambodia". Only use it once the ⭐ results are filled.

**Description.** It is clean and honest, and "No hype. No income promises." is right. Improvements:
- Add one concrete, surprising number as a hook, with the owner's approval: "My first video ads got 2,546 views, 57 clicks and zero bookings. Here is what I learned." Specific failure numbers sell honesty better than adjectives.
- "within a few weeks... for about $31 a month": add "in tool costs". Your own Ch 12 says the real cost is time.
- One of the four "business tools" is the ebook system that wrote this book. Some buyers may find that circular; keep it, but put the booking app and video ads first, as you do.
- Say who it is for in the first lines: "for small-business owners, freelancers and non-programmers anywhere, especially outside the US and Europe."

**Keywords.** These are compliant (no trademarks or author names). Some are too long or have little search demand. Suggestions:
- Keep: "AI for small business owners", "no-code AI tools for beginners", "AI automation for entrepreneurs".
- Replace "real AI projects and costs" with "vibe coding for beginners" (a fast-growing search term in 2026).
- Replace "artificial intelligence guide for non-programmers" with "build apps with AI no programming".
- Replace "AI skills for developing countries" with "AI for entrepreneurs in Asia" or "Telegram mini app" (a descriptive generic term; check KDP's view of product names).

**Categories.** Business › Small Business is right but very competitive. "Computers & Technology › AI & Machine Learning" is dominated by technical books. Consider a less crowded third category, such as "Business & Money › Entrepreneurship" or "Computers & Technology › Web Development & Design" (no-code). Check sales ranks of the #20 book in each candidate category with the same demand method you used in Ch 11, and pick where a few sales a day reach the top 20.

**Price.** $4.99 at launch, rising to $6.99, with KDP Select, is sensible for about 26,000 words (roughly 110 Kindle pages). Competing "AI for small business" Kindle titles cluster at $2.99–$9.99, and the series titles at $2.99–4.99 rely on volume. Given a new author with no platform, consider $2.99–3.99 for the first 2–3 weeks to gather reviews, then $4.99. Do not go above $6.99 until you have 20+ reviews. A paperback (needs an ISBN; KDP gives one free) at about $12.99 makes the ebook price look better value, and many Cambodian and NGO readers prefer print.

**Cover brief.** Good: avoiding robots and money imagery is correct for this positioning.

**Market fit.** Most competitors are prompt lists, "ChatGPT bibles" and income promises, usually by pen names. Your differentiators are a real named person, a real non-Western small business, real numbers including failures, and full AI disclosure. Lean into these everywhere: cover line, first line of the description, the A+ content image of the Telegram booking screen, and the author page. The main competitive weakness is the lack of results and of an author platform. Mitigate both with the "Where things stand" boxes and a simple author page or Telegram channel before launch.
