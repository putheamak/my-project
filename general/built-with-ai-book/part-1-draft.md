# Part One: Why I Started

---

## Chapter 1: The Job That Taught Me What I Didn't Know

For three years, I worked as a research supervisor at a private company in Cambodia, one of the leading consumer brands in its industry. My last day was the 20th of August, 2026.

Before that, I had worked with social NGOs. My background was in **qualitative research**: interviews, focus groups, sitting with people and listening until I understood the story behind their answers. I was good at it, and I loved it.

But the commercial world asks a different question. Management didn't want stories. They wanted **numbers they could trust**: How big is our market share? How many people think of our brand first? Why do customers choose us, or why do they choose our competitor? Answering those questions needs **quantitative research and data analysis**, and I have to be honest: that was not my strongest skill.

So I gave myself a challenge. I would learn the tools that make research reliable, and I would try to automate the whole chain, **from collecting data in the field to a finished report on a manager's desk**.

I taught myself, step by step:

- **KoboToolbox**, so our field teams could collect survey data on their phones instead of paper
- **Excel**, much deeper than I had ever used it before
- **SPSS**, for statistical analysis
- **Python**, my first real contact with code. To be honest, I did not write most of it myself. I described what I needed, an AI wrote the Python code, and I ran it on my own computer to get the results.
- and tools that turn raw data into a **live dashboard**, updated as new data arrives

I didn't know it then, but that last habit, *describing what I need and letting AI write the code*, was the beginning of everything in this book.

### Inside a company that was losing its way

Research gives you an unusual seat in a company. You see how products move, how decisions are made, and how operations run day to day. You see the business from the ground up.

And from that seat, I learned something no textbook had taught me: in many companies, **office politics comes before everything else**. It decides who joins a team, how decisions are made, how plans are carried out, and in the end, the strategy of the company itself.

In my experience there, a small circle of people close to the top had great influence. Decisions changed so often that many staff could not keep up. New employees seemed to be tested less on their work and more on whose side they were on. People who did not fit in found it very hard to stay.

Some time later, I heard an idea on Steven Bartlett's podcast, *The Diary of a CEO*, that explained exactly what I had lived through. A guest argued that large companies almost never catch the big turning points, because the people inside them can't take the career risk. In a big organisation, he said, the most important political skill is simple: **never be wrong on your own.** You can be wrong together with everyone else. You can all jump off the cliff together, and nobody loses their job, because the blame is shared. But if you make a bold decision alone and it fails, you will not receive much mercy.

I have thought about that idea many times. There are two very different ways to be wrong:

- **Being wrong together.** You followed the crowd and did what everyone else did, and it failed. Nobody blames you personally. "Who could have known? Everyone got it wrong." You keep your job.
- **Being wrong alone.** You made an independent call that went against the crowd, and it failed. Now there is a name attached to the failure, and it is yours.

So the "political skill" is to stay with the herd. If things go badly, the group protects you.

The great economist John Maynard Keynes noticed the same thing almost a hundred years ago. In 1936 he wrote that "worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally."¹

The result was a workplace where neither new people nor experienced staff could settle. I was one of them. Slowly, I realised that if things continued like this, I would never give the company the results I was capable of.

### When the data says one thing and management believes another

For a researcher, the most painful thing is watching good data be ignored.

The brand was still one of the leaders in its market. But our research showed clearly that it was not as strong as it used to be. On the measures that matter most, such as being the first brand people think of (**top of mind**), **brand share** and **market position**, the main competitors were getting closer every year.

These findings did not come from a computer model. They came straight from the ground: from real customers and end consumers. But management did not fully trust them. And when leaders don't trust their own data, they can't fix what the data is telling them.

This is where the "never be wrong alone" idea becomes dangerous. Catching a big turning point, like a new technology, a shift in the market, or an unpopular decision that turns out to be right, almost always needs someone to break from the crowd *before* the answer is obvious, while they could still be proven wrong alone. But a political company punishes exactly that. So smart, careful people avoid the independent bet. The organisation stays locked inside its old consensus, and it misses the turn. Not because nobody can see the truth, but because seeing it out loud is personally risky.

The same pattern explains why bubbles last so long, in companies and in whole markets. While the excitement grows, going along with it is the "safe" career move: if it bursts, everyone was wrong together. Warning against it early is lonely. You look foolish while it keeps growing, and you may be pushed out long before you are proven right. There's a famous saying, often credited to Keynes, that "the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent."

In one sentence: **big organisations miss turning points not because nobody sees the truth, but because the people inside are rewarded for agreeing and punished for being right alone.**

Later came the cuts. People in middle and senior management were let go. New people were hired under conditions that, to me, raised serious questions of ethics and professionalism. Some colleagues were pushed out. Some stayed in their comfort zone. Others left to find room to grow.

### Choosing to grow

On the 20th of August, 2026, I chose to leave.

It was not an easy decision. But on that day, I felt more peaceful than I had felt in a long time.

I believe it was the right decision for both sides. The company was not getting the best of me, and I needed space to grow beyond the box I had been working in.

I walked out with two things. The first was a deep understanding of how a business really works, and how good data and good tools can make it far more productive, *if* its leaders are willing to listen. The second was a set of skills I had built by myself: data collection, analysis, dashboards, and a habit of working with AI to write code I could not write alone.

I didn't know that those skills were preparing me for something much bigger. A few years ago, building software meant years of study. Today, people like us, with no computer science degree, can describe a tool in plain words and build it **together with an AI agent**.

This book is about that new version of me. I want to share everything I've learned since that day: what worked, what failed, and what it cost, so that you can do the same.

## Chapter 2: A Warning from Inside the Labs

### "Gambling with our lives"

Less than three weeks after I left my job, on the **8th of September, 2026**, a 27-year-old British researcher named **Jacob Coxon** resigned from Anthropic, one of the world's leading AI companies. He had spent three years doing research at both OpenAI and Anthropic, the companies behind ChatGPT and Claude.

He explained why in a post on X:

> "Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."²

The post spread around the world, reportedly reaching about 76 million views. In an interview with the *Wall Street Journal* the same day, he warned that under the most aggressive scenarios, things could be "out of control" by the end of 2027. TIME, PBS and many others covered the story, and suddenly the whole world was talking about AI risk again.³ That conversation has not stopped since.

I read the news here in Phnom Penh, on my phone, in the middle of building the tools you'll read about in this book. It made me ask a question I still ask myself every day:

**If the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?**

### What is coming

Nobody knows the future. But many AI researchers and company leaders now talk openly about a timeline that would have sounded like science fiction ten years ago.

**Up to about 2030**, most forecasts expect AI to become a normal part of work everywhere:

- AI assistants that write, translate, analyse data and answer customers
- AI "agents" that don't just answer questions but **do tasks**: build software, run processes, manage simple operations
- big changes in office work, customer service, design, marketing, research and programming

**After about 2030**, some of the people closest to the technology believe we could see **superintelligence**: AI systems smarter than humans in almost every area, possibly able to improve themselves. Others believe this is much further away, or will never happen. Honest experts disagree.

What almost everyone agrees on is this: the change will be **big**, and it will be **fast**.

### What it could mean for daily life

Here is how I see the road ahead. This is my own reading of what I have studied and listened to, not a prediction anyone can promise.

**Jobs**

- **2026 to 2028:** AI agents take over routine office tasks. Small businesses gain the most, because one person can now run marketing, accounting and customer support with AI.
- **2028 to 2030:** Many entry-level office jobs shrink. Young graduates compete not only with each other, but with AI. Demand grows for skilled trades, care work, logistics, and anyone who can direct AI and take responsibility for the results.
- **After 2030, if superintelligence arrives:** AI can do most knowledge work. What stays with humans is physical presence, accountability, relationships, local trust, and the decisions people want a human to make.

**Wellbeing**

- **What could get better:** cheaper medicine and diagnosis, free education, cheaper energy from solar, much less paperwork
- **What could get harder:** finding meaning when work changes, loneliness, AI-made scams and fake content, and a growing gap between people who own businesses or assets and people who don't
- **The best protection:** own something (a business, a skill, a group of loyal customers), keep strong family and community ties, stay healthy, and never stop learning

**Cambodia**

- More investment, more e-commerce, and more digital payments such as KHQR
- Our young population is an advantage *only* if young people have skills. Without skills, it becomes a risk.
- Climate, from heat to floods, already affects delivery, farming and health, and AI will not change that on its own

### Three possible futures after 2030

| Scenario | What it looks like | How likely (my opinion) |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Gradual** | AI keeps improving, but cost, law, trust and infrastructure slow it down. It feels like the smartphone era, only faster. | Most likely for Cambodia in the 2030s |
| **B. Fast** | Superintelligence arrives in the early 2030s. Science, medicine and productivity jump. Big countries and companies change within a few years. | Possible |
| **C. Disrupted** | Fast AI combined with economic shocks, job losses, political conflict or misuse. A period of instability. | Possible, and worth preparing for |

### The good and the bad

AI could bring enormous benefits:

- **Health:** faster diagnosis, new medicines, medical advice for people far from a hospital
- **Education:** a personal tutor for every child, in their own language
- **Work and business:** small companies doing what only large companies could do before. This book is one small example.
- **Public services:** faster, cheaper and more transparent government services

And it brings real risks:

- **Jobs:** many tasks done by people today will be done by AI. New jobs will appear, but not always for the same people, in the same places, at the same time.
- **Truth:** fake videos, fake voices and fake news that are hard to tell apart from real ones
- **Power:** a few companies and countries controlling the most powerful technology in history
- **Safety:** the risk that researchers like Jacob Coxon warn about: systems that humans can no longer control

## Chapter 3: The New Divide

### Two worlds, one technology

Here is what worries me most as someone living in a developing country.

In wealthy countries, AI is discussed every day in parliaments, universities, newspapers and podcasts. People there don't know exactly what will happen either, but they are **talking about it, preparing for it, and investing in it**.

In many developing countries, including mine, most people are not yet part of that conversation. Many workers, students, and even business owners have little idea how much their jobs and lives may change. Our economies depend heavily on work that AI and automation may change first, and we have fewer resources to retrain people.

I saw this with my own eyes, even inside one of the biggest companies in the country. Many software systems had been bought and set up, but they were never joined together into one automated flow. Data was collected in one place, cleaned in another, and reported in a third, with people copying between them by hand. The work stayed manual and separate.

Now imagine that company with one or two people skilled in AI, connecting those systems so that data flows from the field to the dashboard by itself. The work of several positions could be done faster, cheaper and with fewer mistakes, and the company would earn more.

That is why I believe this:

**AI will not simply wipe out people's jobs. It will change the way we work, and the people left behind will be those who never learned to work with AI.**

In the past, the "digital divide" meant who had internet access and who did not. The new divide is about who **understands and uses AI**, and who is left behind by it. And this gap is widest between rich and poor countries. In the rich world, many people already use AI to work faster and better. In the poor world, most have not started.

The most serious risk for Cambodia is in **garment and manufacturing jobs**. These jobs were the ladder that lifted many families out of poverty. If robots and AI make factories in rich countries cheap again, poorer countries could lose that ladder. Cambodia needs a Plan B: stronger agriculture value chains, tourism, logistics, digital services, and many more small local businesses.

### A world already under pressure

All of this is happening while the world is already unstable. In Europe, the war between Russia and Ukraine continues. In the Middle East, the war that followed the US and Israeli strikes on Iran in early 2026 has spread. In Yemen, the truce between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia collapsed in July, and attacks on Saudi territory and on shipping have followed.⁴

Wars, energy prices and the race for AI are now connected. Big countries see AI as a matter of national power and security. For small countries like mine, that means the rules of the future may be written by others.

### If superintelligence comes and there aren't enough jobs

This is the hardest question of all, and nobody has a proven answer. The world has never faced anything like it. But economists and governments are already discussing ideas. Here are the main ones, and how I think they apply to a country like Cambodia.

**1. Share the wealth AI creates**

If AI and robots make an economy far more productive, there is more wealth overall. The real problem is who gets it.

- **Tax AI and big technology companies**, on their profits, their computing power, or as a "robot tax", and use the money for people.
- **Universal basic income (UBI):** a regular payment to every citizen, enough for basic needs. It has been tested in places such as Kenya, Finland and the United States, with mixed but often encouraging results.⁵
- **A national AI fund:** the country owns a share of AI and automation profits and uses them for its people. Norway built a huge national fund from its oil wealth, and the US state of Alaska pays every resident a yearly dividend from its oil fund.

**2. Change what "work" means**

- **Shorter working weeks**, so the remaining work is shared among more people
- **Pay for work society needs but markets undervalue:** caring for children, the elderly and the sick, teaching, environmental restoration and community work
- **Public works programmes:** roads, irrigation, flood defences, tree planting and solar installations. These suit Cambodia well, given our climate risks.

**3. Help people move, not just survive**

- **Free, lifelong retraining**, with AI tutors that speak Khmer
- **Support for small businesses:** cheap loans, easy registration and affordable AI tools. In an AI world, many more people will work for themselves.
- **Stronger social protection.** Cambodia already has foundations to build on, such as the IDPoor programme for poor households and the National Social Security Fund (NSSF) for health and pensions.

**4. Spread ownership**

In an AI economy, the people who do best are owners, not employees. So:

- **Cooperatives**, where farmers, riders or sellers own the business together
- Making it easy for ordinary people to own **shares, land and small businesses**
- **Community-owned** solar power, water and internet

**5. Meaning, not just money**

Even with an income, people need purpose, respect and community. Losing a job hurts more than the wallet; it hurts dignity. Families, pagodas, communities, sports, art, volunteering and learning will matter more than ever. This is where NGOs, religious institutions and local communities have a big role to play.

### If it goes badly, and if it goes well

**If it goes badly:**

- **Extreme inequality:** a few companies and countries own the AI, and everyone else depends on them
- **Poor countries left behind:** rich countries can afford basic income, but Cambodia's tax base is much smaller. Global cooperation and fair taxes on AI will matter a lot.
- **Social unrest:** many unemployed young people without hope is a dangerous mix
- **Loss of control:** if superintelligence is not developed safely, the risks go far beyond jobs. That is why AI safety, international rules and careful governments matter.

**If it goes well:**

- Cheaper medicine, food, energy and education for everyone
- Less hard, dangerous and boring work
- More time for family, health, learning and community
- Faster solutions to climate change and disease

The technology will most likely make the world richer overall. But whether ordinary people share that wealth will not be decided by the AI. It will be decided by the choices of governments, companies and citizens like you and me.

## Chapter 4: Why I Built Four Things

### How I decided to respond

For months, I had been listening to people talk about AI: Steven Bartlett's *The Diary of a CEO*, the campaign group ControlAI, CBS's *60 Minutes*, CNN's *Erin Burnett OutFront*, and many others.

The more I listened, the more I believed that big changes are coming soon. And I kept asking myself two questions:

1. **How will I cope with it?**
2. **How can the people around me, here in Cambodia, get ready?**

My answer was not to be afraid, and not to wait. It was to **learn by doing**: to work with AI almost every day, build real things with it, understand both its power and its mistakes, and then share what I learn in simple language.

After leaving my job, I didn't want to only read about AI. I wanted to **use** it to build something real, in a real business, with my own money and time at stake. I gave myself one rule: **each project must solve a real problem, not just be an experiment.**

Here are the four projects, and why each one exists. In Part Two, you'll learn how to build your own version of each.

### 1. A booking app for my delivery business

**Khmer Parcel** is my parcel delivery service in Phnom Penh. It launched on the 1st of October, 2026.

Many of our customers are **online sellers**: people selling clothes, cosmetics or food through Facebook and Telegram. They send many parcels and need to book pickups quickly. Booking through chat messages is slow and messy. Details get lost, prices get confused, and someone has to type everything again.

So I built a **Telegram mini app**. A seller opens our bot, taps one button, fills in a short form in Khmer, and the booking goes straight to our office Telegram group with a booking code. It remembers the shop's details for next time. Every booking is checked, so nobody can post fake orders. And it runs on Cloudflare's free plan, so there is **no server to rent or maintain**.

**Why it matters:** this is AI helping a small local business look and work like a much bigger one.

### 2. A blog that drafts its own posts, which I always check

I wanted to test a promise you hear everywhere: that one person, with AI, can run an online income project on a very small budget.

I chose **affiliate marketing**, because it needs no stock, no warehouse and no shipping. You recommend products, and if a reader buys through your link, the store pays you a small commission. It is free to join, which made it a fair test for someone starting with little money. I joined Amazon's program, because so many people already shop there.

For the topic, I chose **kitchen gadgets under $30**. Small, useful things that people buy without thinking for weeks. Everyone has a kitchen, the topic never goes out of date, and it is very visual, which suits Pinterest and short videos. The price limit also keeps the blog focused and easy to trust.

With AI, I built a system that, three times a week, picks a topic, **writes a draft post**, prepares social media captions and video scripts, and **checks the draft against Amazon's rules**. Nothing is published until I read it and approve it.

**Why it matters:** it tests the most common promise of AI, "passive income", in an honest way. At the time of writing, the blog has not made its first sale. I will tell you exactly what happens, good or bad.

### 3. Video ads every day, without a video editor

A new business needs to be seen. Facebook and TikTok reward people who post often, but I couldn't afford a video editor or an agency.

So, with AI, I built a small **video factory**. Each ad is designed like a simple web page. A browser plays it and records it frame by frame, and the frames are joined into a vertical video ready for Reels and TikTok, with Khmer text first and short English underneath.

To make a new ad, I copy an earlier design, change the words and pictures, check a quick preview of each scene, and then render the full video. No editing timeline, no expensive software. Most of my time now goes into deciding *what to say*, not into the editing itself.

**Why it matters:** marketing that used to need a team can now be done by one person.

### 4. An ebook machine, which helped make this book

The fourth project is the one you are reading. With AI, I built a step-by-step system: **research a topic → check demand on Amazon → outline → draft chapters → human edit → format → publish**.

In Part Two, you'll see that my first two book ideas, a Phnom Penh travel guide and a guide for expats, failed the demand check. That failure is part of the lesson.

**Why it matters:** it shows how AI can help you create and sell knowledge, as long as a human stays in charge of the quality and the truth.

### What these four have in common

None of these projects needed me to become a programmer. Each one started with a **problem I could describe in plain words**. Each was built in conversation with an AI agent: I explained, it built, I tested, it fixed. And each one taught me where AI is brilliant, and where it makes mistakes that only a human will catch.

That is the skill I believe everyone will need in the coming years, in Cambodia and everywhere else. It isn't about knowing how to code. It's about **knowing how to work with AI**.

In Part Two, I'll show you the tools I use, what they cost, how to talk to AI so it builds what you actually need, and then each project, step by step.
