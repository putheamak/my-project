## Chapter 1 — The job that taught me what I didn't know

I used to be a research supervisor at a private company in Cambodia. ⭐ [Add: how many years, and the sector in general words only, e.g. "consumer goods". Do not name the company.]

My training was mostly in **qualitative research**: interviews, focus groups, listening to people and finding the story behind their answers. I was good at it. But the company needed more than stories. Management wanted numbers they could trust: market share, brand awareness, why customers chose us or a competitor. That meant **quantitative research and data analysis**, and that was not my strongest skill.

So I set myself a goal. I would learn the tools that could make our research more reliable, and I would try to automate the whole chain, **from collecting data in the field to a report on a manager's desk**. I taught myself step by step:

- **KoboToolbox**, to collect survey data on phones instead of paper
- **Excel**, much deeper than before
- **SPSS**, for statistical analysis
- **Python**, my first real contact with code
- and tools that could turn raw data into a **live dashboard**, updated as the data came in

I did not know it then, but this was the beginning of everything in this book.

### Inside a company that was losing its way

Working in research gave me an unusual view of the company. I saw how the business worked: how products moved, how decisions were made, and how operations ran day to day.

I also learned something that no textbook had taught me. In many companies, **office politics comes before everything else**. It shaped who joined a team, how decisions were made, how plans were carried out, and in the end, the company's strategy itself.

In my experience there, a small circle of people close to the top had great influence. Decisions changed so often that many staff could not keep up. New employees seemed to be tested less on their work and more on whose side they were on. People who did not fit in found it hard to stay. ⭐ [Keep this general. Avoid any detail that could identify a person or the company.]

The result was a working environment where neither new people nor experienced staff could settle. I was one of them. I realised that if things continued like this, I could not give the company the results I was capable of.

### When the data says one thing and management believes another

The most painful part, for a researcher, was watching the data be ignored.

The brand was still one of the leaders in its market. But our research showed that it was not as strong as before. On the measures that matter, such as being the first brand people think of (**top of mind**), **brand share** and **market position**, the main competitors were getting closer every year. ⭐ [Optional: one general example, without real figures.]

The findings came straight from the ground, from real customers. But management did not fully trust them. And when leaders don't trust their own data, they can't fix what the data is telling them.

Later came cuts. People in middle and senior management were let go. New people were hired under conditions that, to me, raised questions of ethics and professionalism. Some colleagues were pushed out. Some stayed in their comfort zone. Others left to find room to grow.

### Choosing to grow

I chose to leave. ⭐ [Add: month/year you left, and how you felt that day.]

It was not an easy decision, but I believe it was right for both sides. The company was not getting the best of me, and I needed space to grow beyond the box I had been working in.

I left with two things. First, a deep understanding of how a business really works, and how good data and good tools could make it more productive, if leaders are willing to listen. Second, a set of skills I had built by myself: data collection, analysis, dashboards, a little Python.

I didn't know that those skills were preparing me for something much bigger. A few years ago, building software meant years of study. Today, someone like me, with no computer science degree, can describe a tool in plain words and build it **together with an AI agent**.

This book is about that new version of me. I want to share everything I've learned: what worked, what failed, and what it cost, so that you can do the same.

## Chapter 2 — The world is changing faster than we are

### "Gambling with our lives"

On **8 September 2026**, a 27-year-old British researcher named **Jacob Coxon** resigned from Anthropic, one of the world's leading AI companies. He had spent three years doing research at both OpenAI and Anthropic, the companies behind ChatGPT and Claude.

He explained why in a post on X:

> "Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."

The post spread around the world, reportedly reaching about 76 million views. In an interview with the *Wall Street Journal* the same day, he warned that things could be "out of control" by the end of 2027 under the most aggressive scenarios. Major news outlets, including TIME and PBS, covered the story, and the world was suddenly talking about AI risk again. That conversation has not stopped since.

I read about it here in Phnom Penh, on my phone, between building the tools you'll read about in this book. It made me ask a question I still ask myself every day:

**If the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?**

### What is coming: before and after 2030

Nobody knows the future. But many AI researchers and company leaders now talk openly about a timeline that would have sounded like science fiction ten years ago.

**Up to about 2030**, most forecasts expect AI to become a normal part of work everywhere:

- AI assistants that write, translate, analyse data and answer customers
- AI "agents" that don't just answer questions but **do tasks**: build software, run processes, manage simple operations
- big changes in office work, customer service, design, marketing, research and programming

**After about 2030**, some of the people closest to the technology believe we could see **superintelligence**: AI systems smarter than humans in almost every area, possibly able to improve themselves. Others think this is much further away, or will never happen. Honest people disagree. [CHECK: keep this balanced; no specific predictions in anyone's name unless we quote a source.]

What almost everyone agrees on is that the change will be **big**, and it will be **fast**.

### The good and the bad

AI could bring enormous benefits:

- **Health:** faster diagnosis, new medicines, medical advice for people far from a hospital
- **Education:** a personal tutor for every child, in their own language
- **Work and business:** small companies doing what only large companies could do before. This book is an example.
- **Public services:** faster, cheaper and more transparent government services

And it brings real risks:

- **Jobs:** many tasks done by people today will be done by AI. New jobs will appear, but not always for the same people, in the same places, at the same time.
- **Truth:** fake videos, fake voices and fake news that are hard to tell apart from real ones
- **Power:** a few companies and countries controlling the most powerful technology in history
- **Safety:** the risk that researchers like Jacob Coxon warn about, systems that humans can no longer control

### The new divide

Here is what worries me most as someone living in a developing country.

In wealthy countries, AI is discussed every day in parliaments, universities, newspapers and podcasts. People there don't know exactly what will happen, but they are **talking about it, preparing, and investing**.

In many developing countries, including mine, most people are not yet part of that conversation. Many workers, students, and even business owners have little idea how much their jobs and lives may change. Our economies rely heavily on work that AI and automation may change first, and we have fewer resources to retrain people. ⭐ [Optional: one or two examples from Cambodia you have seen yourself.]

This is a new kind of gap. In the past, the "digital divide" meant who had internet access. Now it means who **understands and uses AI**, and who is left behind by it.

### A world already under pressure

All of this is happening while the world is already unstable. The war between Russia and Ukraine continues in Europe. In the Middle East, the war that followed the US and Israeli strikes on Iran in early 2026 has spread: in Yemen, the truce between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia collapsed in July, and attacks on Saudi territory and shipping have followed.

Wars, energy prices and the race for AI are now connected. Countries see AI as a matter of national power and security. For small countries, that means the rules of the future may be written by others.

### How I decided to respond

I have spent many hours listening to people talk about AI: Steven Bartlett's *The Diary of a CEO*, the campaign group ControlAI, CBS's *60 Minutes*, CNN's *Erin Burnett OutFront*, and others. ⭐ [CHECK: you also mentioned "Jamal Bryant" and "Uncover AI". Please confirm the exact names of these shows.]

The more I listened, the more I believed that big changes are coming soon. And I asked myself two questions:

1. **How will I cope with it?**
2. **How can the people around me, here in Cambodia, get ready?**

My answer was not to be afraid, and not to wait. It was to **learn by doing**: to work with AI almost every day, build real things with it, understand both its power and its mistakes, and then share what I learn in simple language.

That is why I started building. And that is why I wrote this book.

## Chapter 3 — Why I built four things

After leaving my job, I didn't want to only read about AI. I wanted to **use** it to build something real, in a real business, with my own money and time at stake.

I gave myself one rule: **each project must solve a real problem, not just be an experiment.** Here are the four, and why each one exists. You'll learn how to build your own version of each in Part 2.

### 1. A booking app for my delivery business

**Khmer Parcel** is my parcel delivery service in Phnom Penh. It launched on 1 October 2026.

Many of our customers are **online sellers**: people selling clothes, cosmetics or food through Facebook and Telegram. They need to send many parcels and book pickups quickly. Booking through chat messages is slow and messy: details get lost, prices get confused, and someone has to type everything again. ⭐ [Add your real experience of this problem.]

So I built a **Telegram mini app**. A seller opens our bot, taps one button, fills a short form in Khmer, and the booking goes straight to our office Telegram group with a booking code. It remembers the shop's details for next time. Every booking is checked, so nobody can post fake orders. It runs on Cloudflare's free plan, so there is **no server to rent or maintain**.

**Why it matters:** this is AI helping a small local business look and work like a bigger one.

### 2. A blog that drafts its own posts, which I always check

I wanted to test whether one person could run an **online income project** on a very small budget. I chose an Amazon affiliate blog about useful kitchen gadgets under $30.

With AI, I built a system that, three times a week, researches a topic, **writes a draft post**, prepares social media captions and video scripts, and **checks the draft against Amazon's rules**. Nothing is published until I read it and approve it. ⭐ [Add: why this niche, and why affiliate marketing.]

**Why it matters:** it tests the most common promise of AI, "passive income", in an honest way. I'll tell you exactly what happens, including that at the time of writing it has not made a sale yet. ⭐ [Update with real numbers before publishing.]

### 3. Video ads every day, without a video editor

A new business needs to be seen. Facebook and TikTok reward people who post often, but I couldn't afford a video editor or an agency.

So with AI, I built a small **video factory**. Each ad is designed like a simple web page, a browser records it frame by frame, and the frames are joined into a vertical video ready for Reels and TikTok, with Khmer text first and short English. To make a new ad, I copy an old design, change the words, and render it.

**Why it matters:** marketing that used to need a team can now be done by one person in a short time. ⭐ [Add: how many minutes one ad takes you now.]

### 4. An ebook machine, which helped make this book

The fourth project is the one in your hands. I built a step-by-step system with AI: **research a topic → check demand on Amazon → outline → draft chapters → human edit → format → publish**.

You'll see in Part 2 that my first two book ideas, a Phnom Penh travel guide and an expat guide, failed the demand check. That failure is part of the lesson.

**Why it matters:** it shows how AI can help you create and sell knowledge, as long as a human stays in charge of the quality and the truth.

### What these four have in common

None of these projects needed me to become a programmer. Each one started with a **problem I could describe in plain words**. Each was built in conversation with an AI agent: I explained, it built, I tested, it fixed. And each one taught me where AI is brilliant and where it makes mistakes that only a human will catch.

That is the skill I believe everyone will need in the coming years, in Cambodia and everywhere else. It's not about knowing how to code. It's about **knowing how to work with AI**.

In the next chapters I'll show you the tools I use, what they cost, and how to talk to AI so it builds what you actually need.
