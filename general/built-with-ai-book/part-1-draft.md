# Part One: Why I Started

---

## Chapter 1: The Job That Taught Me What I Didn't Know

On the 20th of August, 2026, I walked out of my office for the last time.

For three years, I had worked as a research supervisor at a private company in Cambodia, one of the leading consumer brands in its industry. I did not leave because of one bad day. I left because, slowly, I had come to understand something about how large organisations work, and about how the world of work itself was about to change.

This chapter is about that understanding. It is also where my story with AI begins.

### From stories to numbers

I studied computer science at university, majoring in networking, but I never trained as a programmer or a data analyst. Before I entered the corporate world, I worked with social NGOs, where my work was in **qualitative research**: interviews, focus groups, and long conversations with people until I understood the story behind their answers. I was good at it, and I loved it.

The commercial world asked a different kind of question. Management did not want stories; they wanted **numbers they could trust**. How large is our market share? How many people think of our brand first? Why do customers choose us, and why do some of them choose our competitors? Answering those questions requires **quantitative research and data analysis**, and I have to be honest: that was not my strongest skill.

So I set myself a challenge. I would master the tools that make research reliable, and I would try to automate the entire chain, **from collecting data in the field to a finished report on a manager's desk**.

I taught myself one tool at a time. **KoboToolbox** let our field teams collect survey data on their phones instead of on paper. **Excel** became far more than a spreadsheet to me. **SPSS** handled the statistical analysis. Then came **Python**, my first real contact with code. In truth, I wrote very little of it myself. I described what I needed, an AI assistant wrote the Python code, and I ran it on my own computer to produce the results. Finally, I learned tools that turn raw data into a **live dashboard**, updated automatically as new data arrives.

I did not realise it at the time, but that quiet habit, *describing what I need and letting AI write the code*, was the beginning of everything in this book.

### Inside a company that was losing its way

Research gives you an unusual seat in a company. You see how products move, how decisions are made, and how operations run from day to day. You see the business from the ground up.

From that seat, I learned something no textbook had taught me: in many companies, **politics comes before performance**. It decides who joins a team, how decisions are made, how plans are carried out, and ultimately, the strategy of the company itself.

In my experience, a small circle of people close to the top held great influence. Decisions changed so often that many staff could not keep up. New employees seemed to be judged less on their work than on whose side they were on, and those who did not fit in found it very hard to stay.

Months later, I heard an interview that put words to what I had lived through. The veteran investor **Jeremy Grantham**, co-founder of the investment firm GMO, appeared on Steven Bartlett's podcast *The Diary of a CEO*. For decades, Grantham has spoken about what he calls *career risk*. His argument, as I understood it, goes like this: large organisations almost never catch the big turning points, because the people inside them cannot afford the career risk involved. In a big institution, the most valuable political skill is simple: **never be wrong on your own.** You can be wrong together with everyone else; you can all jump off the cliff together, and nobody loses their job, because the blame is shared. But if you make a bold call alone and it fails, you will receive very little mercy.¹

I have thought about this idea many times since. There are two very different ways to be wrong:

- **Wrong together.** You followed the crowd, did what everyone else did, and it failed. Nobody blames you personally. *Who could have known? Everyone got it wrong.* You keep your job.
- **Wrong alone.** You made an independent call against the crowd, and it failed. Now the failure has a name attached to it, and the name is yours.

The rational political strategy, then, is to stay with the herd. If things go badly, the group protects you.

None of this is new. Almost a century ago, the economist John Maynard Keynes wrote that "worldly wisdom teaches that it is better for reputation to fail conventionally than to succeed unconventionally."²

The result, where I worked, was an environment in which neither newcomers nor experienced staff could settle. I was one of them. Gradually, I accepted that if nothing changed, I would never give the company the results I knew I was capable of.

### When the data says one thing and management believes another

For a researcher, nothing is more painful than watching good data be ignored.

The brand was still one of the leaders in its market. But our research showed clearly that it was not as strong as it had been. On the measures that matter most, being the first brand people think of (**top of mind**), **brand share**, and **market position**, the main competitors were getting closer every year.

These findings did not come from a model on a computer. They came straight from the ground, from real customers and end consumers. Yet management did not fully trust them. And when leaders do not trust their own data, they cannot fix what the data is telling them.

This is where "never be wrong alone" becomes dangerous. Catching a turning point, whether a new technology, a shift in the market, or an unpopular decision that later proves right, almost always requires someone to break from the consensus *before* the answer is obvious, while they can still be proven wrong on their own. A political organisation punishes exactly that behaviour. So capable, careful people avoid the independent bet, the organisation stays locked inside its old assumptions, and it misses the turn. Not because nobody can see the truth, but because saying it out loud is personally dangerous.

The same logic explains why bubbles last so long, inside companies and across entire markets. While the excitement grows, joining in is the safe career move: if it bursts, everyone was wrong together. Warning against it early is lonely. You look foolish while it keeps growing, and you may be pushed aside long before you are proven right. It is no coincidence that Grantham, a man who built his reputation by calling bubbles early, used that same interview to warn that today's excitement around AI stocks may itself be a bubble.³ There is a famous line, often credited to Keynes, that captures the danger: "the market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent."²

In one sentence: **big organisations miss turning points not because nobody sees the truth, but because the people inside them are rewarded for agreeing and punished for being right alone.**

Then came the cuts. People in middle and senior management were let go. New people were hired under conditions that, in my view, raised serious questions of ethics and professionalism. Some colleagues were pushed out. Some stayed within their comfort zone. Others left to find room to grow.

### Choosing to grow

I chose to leave.

It was not an easy decision. Yet on that last day, I felt more peaceful than ever.

I believe the decision was right for both sides. The company was not getting the best of me, and I needed space to grow beyond the box I had been working in.

I left with two things. The first was a deep understanding of how a business really works, and of how good data and good tools can make it far more productive, *if* its leaders are willing to listen. The second was a set of skills I had built on my own: data collection, analysis, dashboards, and the habit of working with AI to write code I could not write alone.

I did not know that those skills were preparing me for something much bigger. Only a few years ago, building software required years of study. Today, people like us, who are not professional programmers, can describe a tool in plain language and build it **together with an AI agent**.

This book is about that new version of me. In the pages that follow, I will share everything I have learned since that August day: what worked, what failed, and what it cost, so that you can do the same.

## Chapter 2: A Warning from Inside the Labs

### "Gambling with our lives"

Less than three weeks after I left my job, on the **8th of September, 2026**, a 27-year-old British researcher named **Jacob Coxon** resigned from Anthropic, one of the world's leading artificial intelligence companies. He had spent three years doing research at both OpenAI and Anthropic, the companies behind ChatGPT and Claude.

He explained his decision in a post on X:

> "Neither company is acting responsibly. They are racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives."⁴

The post spread around the world, reportedly reaching about 76 million views. In an interview with the *Wall Street Journal* that same day, Coxon warned that under the most aggressive scenarios, events could be "out of control" by the end of 2027. TIME, PBS and many other outlets covered the story, and almost overnight the world was talking about AI risk again.⁵ That conversation has not stopped since.

I read the news here in Phnom Penh, on my phone, in the middle of building the tools you will read about in this book. It left me with a question I still ask myself every day:

**If the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?**

### What is coming

No one knows the future. But we no longer have to guess blindly. Researchers, international organisations and employers have measured where AI is heading, and their numbers point in the same direction.

**Until around 2030: AI joins almost every job.** AI assistants already write, translate, analyse data and answer customers. AI *agents* go further: they **carry out tasks**, such as building software, filling in forms and running simple operations. The numbers show how wide this reaches:

- The **International Monetary Fund (IMF)** estimates that almost **40% of jobs worldwide** are exposed to AI, rising to about **60% in advanced economies**.⁶
- In the **World Economic Forum's** 2025 survey of more than 1,000 large employers, companies expected **22% of today's jobs** to be disrupted by 2030: about **170 million new jobs** created and **92 million** lost. They also expected **39% of workers' core skills** to change.⁷
- The money is moving just as fast. According to Stanford University's *AI Index 2026*, companies around the world invested about **$582 billion** in AI in 2025, up **130%** in a single year.⁸

**After around 2030: possibly superintelligence.** Some of the people closest to the technology believe AI may become more capable than humans in almost every field, and perhaps able to improve itself. Others think this is far away, or will never happen. In the largest survey of its kind, **2,778 AI researchers** gave a **10% chance** that machines could outperform humans at every task by **2027**, and a **50% chance by 2047**. That 2047 estimate came **13 years earlier** than the same survey found just one year before.⁹

So serious experts disagree about *when*. But on two points almost all of them agree: the change will be **large**, and the forecasts keep moving **closer**.

### What it could mean for daily life

What follows is my own reading of what I have studied. It is a perspective, not a promise.

**Work.** Between 2026 and 2028, AI agents will likely take over much routine office work. Small businesses may gain the most, because one person with AI can now handle marketing, accounts and customer support. By 2030, many **entry-level office jobs** may shrink, and young graduates will compete not only with each other but with AI. Demand will grow for skilled trades, care work, logistics, and people who can direct AI and take responsibility for the result. If superintelligence arrives, what stays human will be presence, accountability, relationships and trust.

**Wellbeing.** Some things could get much better: cheaper diagnosis and medicine, free education, cheaper solar energy, less paperwork. Others could get harder: loneliness, AI-made scams and fake videos, and a wider gap between people who own something and people who do not. My advice to myself: own something (a business, a skill or loyal customers), keep family and community close, stay healthy, and never stop learning.

**Cambodia.** I expect more investment, more e-commerce and more digital payments such as KHQR. Our young population is a great advantage, but *only* if young people have the right skills. Without them, it becomes a risk. Chapter 3 looks at that risk in detail.

### Three possible futures after 2030

| Scenario | What it looks like | How likely (my opinion) |
|---|---|---|
| **A. Gradual** | AI keeps improving, but cost, rules, trust and infrastructure slow it down. It feels like the smartphone era, only faster. | Most likely for Cambodia in the 2030s |
| **B. Fast** | Superintelligence arrives in the early 2030s. Science, medicine and productivity leap forward within a few years. | Possible |
| **C. Disrupted** | Fast AI progress collides with job losses, economic shocks, conflict or misuse, and brings a period of instability. | Possible, and worth preparing for |

### The promise and the danger

The benefits could be enormous: faster diagnosis and new medicines, a personal tutor for every child in their own language, small companies doing what only big ones could do before (this book is one small example), and faster, cheaper public services.

The risks are just as real. New **jobs** will appear, but not always for the same people, in the same places, at the same time. **Truth** is under pressure, as fake videos, voices and news become hard to tell from real ones. **Power** may gather in the hands of a few companies and countries. And there is **safety**, the danger Jacob Coxon warned about: systems that humans can no longer control.

## Chapter 3: The New Divide

### Two worlds, one technology

What worries me most, as someone living in a developing country, is not the technology itself. It is who will be ready for it.

In wealthy countries, AI is debated every day in parliaments, universities, newspapers and podcasts. People there do not know exactly what will happen either, but they are **talking about it, preparing for it and investing in it**. In many developing countries, including my own, most people are not yet part of that conversation. Many workers, students and even business owners have little idea how much their jobs and lives may change.

In the past, the "digital divide" meant who had internet access and who did not. The new divide is about who **understands and uses AI**, and who is left behind by it. This is not only my feeling. The numbers show it clearly.

### The real gap, in numbers

**1. Getting online.** About **2.2 billion people** are still offline, and **96%** of them live in low- and middle-income countries. Where people are online, the gap continues: a user in a high-income country uses about **eight times** more mobile data than a user in a low-income country, and basic mobile broadband is still unaffordable in about **60%** of low- and middle-income countries.¹⁰ In Cambodia, about **67%** of people were using the internet at the end of 2025. That is real progress, but it still means roughly **one Cambodian in three** is not online at all.¹¹ You cannot use AI without the internet.

**2. Using AI.** Microsoft tracks how many working-age people use generative AI tools. In mid-2026, the figure was about **29% in the Global North** but only **16% in the Global South**, and the gap is **growing, not shrinking**.¹² The leaders are not only the richest Western countries: in the United Arab Emirates and Singapore, around **six in ten** working-age people already use AI. Countries that invest in skills can move fast.

**3. Money.** In 2025, private investors put about **$286 billion** into AI in the United States alone, roughly **23 times** more than in China, the world's second-largest AI power.¹³ The United Nations' trade body, UNCTAD, found that just **100 companies**, mostly in the United States and China, account for **40%** of the world's private spending on research and development.¹⁴ The money that shapes AI is being spent in very few places.

**4. Machines.** Modern AI runs on huge data centres full of specialised chips. A 2025 University of Oxford study found that only **32 countries** host these AI data centres, and more than **150 countries** have none. Most are run by American and Chinese companies.¹⁵ A country without them must rent computing power from abroad, at foreign prices and under foreign laws.

**5. Rules.** UNCTAD also found that **118 countries**, mostly in the Global South, are absent from the major international discussions on how AI should be governed, and fewer than **one in three** developing countries has a national AI strategy.¹⁴ The rules for the most powerful technology in history are being written mostly without us. There is good news here too: in the Government AI Readiness Index, Cambodia rose from **145th** in 2024 to **118th** of 195 countries in 2025.¹⁶ We are moving, but from far behind.

**6. Language.** AI learns from text on the internet, and most of that text is in English. Khmer is what researchers call a **low-resource language**: there is far less Khmer text available, so AI tools usually understand and write Khmer less well than English.¹⁷ For a farmer or a market seller who reads only Khmer, the best AI tools are still much harder to use.

Put these six gaps together and the picture is clear. The new divide is not one gap. It is **six gaps stacked on top of each other**: access, use, money, machines, rules and language.

### The strange part: less exposed today, more at risk tomorrow

Here is something that surprised me. The IMF found that AI touches about **60% of jobs in advanced economies**, but only about **40% in emerging economies** and **26% in low-income countries**.⁶ At first, this sounds like good news for poorer countries: fewer of our jobs are in AI's way.

But the IMF itself warned about the other side. Poorer countries also **lack the infrastructure and skilled workers to benefit from AI**, so over time AI is likely to make the **inequality between countries worse**.⁶ Rich countries will use AI to become more productive and cheaper. If we stay where we are, our main advantage, lower wages, becomes less valuable every year.

I have seen a small version of this with my own eyes, inside one of the largest companies in the country. Several software systems had been bought and set up, but they were never connected. Data was collected in one place, cleaned in another and reported in a third, with people copying it between systems by hand. Now imagine the same company with just one or two people skilled in AI, connecting those systems so that data flows from the field to the dashboard on its own. The work would be faster, cheaper and more accurate.

That experience shaped a belief I hold firmly:

**AI will not simply wipe out jobs. It will change the way we work, and the people left behind will be those who never learned to work with it.**

### Cambodia's ladder

For Cambodia, the most serious risk lies in **garment and manufacturing jobs**. In 2025, garments, footwear and travel goods earned Cambodia about **$15.5 billion**, roughly **half of all the country's exports**. The sector employs more than **one million workers**, and about **three in four of them are women**.¹⁸

These jobs have been the ladder that lifted many families out of poverty. Many of the workers send money home to their parents and children in the provinces. And this ladder is fragile. In 2016, the International Labour Organization estimated that **88%** of Cambodia's salaried garment and footwear workers were in jobs at **high risk of automation**, one of the highest rates in Southeast Asia.¹⁹ That estimate is ten years old, and the change has been slower than it predicted. But the direction has not changed, and AI and robotics have only grown stronger since.

Office jobs are exposed in a different way. A 2025 ILO study found that women are about **twice as likely** as men to work in jobs highly exposed to generative AI, mainly because so many women work in clerical and administrative roles.²⁰ In Cambodia, that means the same families could be hit twice: daughters in the factory, and daughters in the office.

If robots and AI make manufacturing cheap again in rich countries, or in a few highly automated ones, poorer countries could lose that ladder. Cambodia needs a Plan B: stronger agricultural value chains, tourism, logistics, digital services, and many more small local businesses.

### If AI arrives sooner, or later

No one knows the exact timing. But we can think through both cases.

**If AI arrives sooner** (strong AI agents spread widely before 2030):

- **The gap grows fastest.** Rich countries and companies, which already use AI the most, pull further ahead before most of the developing world has even started.
- **Outsourced work disappears first.** Data entry, customer support, translation and simple design, the kinds of work many developing countries earn money from, are exactly what AI does well.
- **Young graduates are hit hardest.** Entry-level jobs, the first step of a career, are the easiest to automate. A country with a young population, like ours, could face many educated young people without work.
- **Factories may move.** If automated factories near rich markets become cheaper than workers here, orders may leave, and with them the ladder described above.
- **Scams and fake content spread** faster than people learn to recognise them, and those with the least digital experience are the easiest to fool.
- **There is no time to prepare.** Schools, laws and social protection change slowly. Technology does not wait.

**If AI arrives later** (gradually, over the 2030s and beyond):

- **We gain time**, the most valuable thing a developing country can have right now.
- **That time is only useful if we use it**: to put internet in every home, AI training in every school and university, Khmer-language AI tools, and clear rules to protect people.
- **The danger is comfort.** A slow change feels like no change. If we wait, the gap still grows every year, only more quietly, and when the big change finally arrives we will be no more ready than today.

Either way, the cost of waiting is the same: **the later we start, the wider the gap we will have to close.**

### A world already under pressure

All of this is unfolding in a world that is already unstable. In Europe, the war between Russia and Ukraine continues. In the Middle East, the war that followed the US and Israeli strikes on Iran in early 2026 has spread; in Yemen, the truce between the Houthis and Saudi Arabia collapsed in July, followed by attacks on Saudi territory and on shipping.²¹

Wars, energy prices and the race for AI are now deeply connected. Major powers see AI as a question of national strength and security. For small countries like mine, this means the rules of the future may well be written by others.

### If superintelligence comes and there are not enough jobs

This is the hardest question of all, and no one has a proven answer. The world has never faced anything like it. Economists and governments are, however, already discussing possible responses. Here are the main ideas, and how I believe they apply to a country like Cambodia.

**1. Share the wealth AI creates.** If AI and robots make economies far more productive, there will be more wealth overall; the real question is who receives it. Governments could tax AI and large technology companies, whether on profits, on computing power or through a "robot tax", and use the revenue for their citizens. Another option is a **universal basic income**: a regular payment to every citizen, enough to cover basic needs. It has been tested in Kenya, Finland and the United States, with mixed but often encouraging results.²² A third option is a **national AI fund**, in which the country owns a share of automation's profits and uses them for its people. Norway built one of the world's largest national funds from its oil wealth, and the US state of Alaska pays every resident a yearly dividend from its own oil fund.

**2. Redefine what "work" means.** Shorter working weeks would share the remaining work among more people. Societies could begin paying for work that markets undervalue, such as caring for children, the elderly and the sick, teaching, environmental restoration and community service. Public works programmes, including roads, irrigation, flood defences, tree planting and solar installations, would suit Cambodia particularly well, given our climate risks.

**3. Help people move forward, not merely survive.** This means free, lifelong retraining, delivered through AI tutors that speak Khmer; support for small businesses through affordable loans, simple registration and low-cost AI tools, since in an AI world many more people will work for themselves; and stronger social protection. Cambodia already has foundations to build on, such as the IDPoor programme for poor households and the National Social Security Fund for health care and pensions.

**4. Spread ownership.** In an AI economy, owners will fare better than employees. Cooperatives, in which farmers, riders or sellers own a business together, can help. So can making it easier for ordinary people to own shares, land and small businesses, and building community-owned solar power, water and internet services.

**5. Protect meaning, not just income.** Even with an income, people need purpose, respect and belonging. Losing a job hurts more than the wallet; it hurts dignity. Families, pagodas, communities, sports, the arts, volunteering and learning will matter more than ever, and NGOs, religious institutions and local communities will have a vital role to play.

### Two possible endings

If this transition goes badly, we could see **extreme inequality**, with a handful of companies and countries owning the AI while everyone else depends on them. **Poor countries could be left behind**: rich nations can afford a basic income, but Cambodia's tax base is far smaller, so global cooperation and fair taxation of AI will matter enormously. Large numbers of unemployed young people without hope could lead to **social unrest**. And if superintelligence is not developed safely, we risk a **loss of control** whose consequences go far beyond jobs, which is why AI safety, international rules and careful governments matter.

If it goes well, however, the picture is very different: cheaper medicine, food, energy and education for everyone; less hard, dangerous and tedious work; more time for family, health, learning and community; and faster solutions to climate change and disease.

The technology will most likely make the world richer overall. But whether ordinary people share in that wealth will not be decided by AI. It will be decided by the choices of governments, companies, and citizens like you and me.

## Chapter 4: Why I Built Four Things

### How I decided to respond

For months, I had been listening to people talk about AI: Steven Bartlett's *The Diary of a CEO*, the campaign group ControlAI, CBS's *60 Minutes*, CNN's *Erin Burnett OutFront*, and many others.

The more I listened, the more convinced I became. I am a believer: AI is coming, and sooner than many of us expect. Two questions stayed with me:

1. **How will I cope with it?**
2. **How can the people around me, here in Cambodia, prepare?**

My answer was neither to be afraid nor to wait. It was to **learn by doing**: to work with AI almost every day, build real things with it, understand both its power and its mistakes, and then share what I learn in simple language.

After leaving my job, I did not want only to read about AI. I wanted to **use** it, to build something real, in a real business, with my own money and time at stake. I set myself one rule: **every project must solve a real problem; none can be a mere experiment.**

Here are the four projects, and why each one exists. In Part Two, you will learn how to build your own version of each.

### 1. A booking app for my delivery business

**Khmer Parcel** is my parcel delivery service in Phnom Penh. It launched on the 1st of October, 2026.

Many of our customers are **online sellers**, people selling clothes, cosmetics or food through Facebook and Telegram. They send many parcels and need to book pickups quickly. Booking through chat messages is slow and messy: details get lost, prices get confused, and someone has to type everything out again.

So I built a **Telegram mini app**. A seller opens our bot, taps a single button, fills in a short form in Khmer, and the booking goes straight to our office Telegram group with a booking code. The app remembers each shop's details for next time, and every booking is verified, so no one can post fake orders. It runs on Cloudflare's free plan, which means there is **no server to rent or maintain**.

This is AI helping a small local business look and work like a much larger one.

### 2. A blog that drafts its own posts, which I always check

I wanted to test a promise heard everywhere: that one person, with AI, can run an online income project on a very small budget.

I chose **affiliate marketing**, because it requires no stock, no warehouse and no shipping. You recommend products, and when a reader buys through your link, the store pays you a small commission. It is free to join, which made it a fair test for someone starting with little money. I joined Amazon's program, because so many people already shop there.

The blog is called **Home Picks Under 30**, and it focuses on kitchen gadgets under $30: small, useful things people buy without much hesitation. Everyone has a kitchen, the topic never goes out of date, and it is highly visual, which suits Pinterest and short videos. The price limit keeps the blog focused and easy to trust.

With AI, I built a system that, three times a week, chooses a topic, **writes a draft post**, prepares social media captions and video scripts, and **checks the draft against Amazon's rules**. Nothing is published until I have read and approved it.

It tests the most common promise made about AI, "passive income", in an honest way. At the time of writing, the blog has not yet made its first sale. I will tell you exactly what happens, good or bad.

### 3. Video ads every day, without a video editor

A new business needs to be seen. Facebook and TikTok reward people who post often, but I could not afford a video editor or an agency.

So, with AI, I built a small **video factory**. Each ad is designed like a simple web page. A browser plays it and records it frame by frame, and the frames are assembled into a vertical video, 30 to 60 seconds long, ready for Reels and TikTok, with Khmer text first and short English beneath it.

To make a new ad, I copy an earlier design, change the words and images, check a quick preview of each scene, and render the final video. There is no editing timeline and no expensive software. Most of my time now goes into deciding *what to say*, not into editing.

Marketing that once required a team can now be done by one person.

### 4. An ebook machine, which helped make this book

The fourth project is the one you are reading. With AI, I built a step-by-step system: **research a topic → check demand on Amazon → outline → draft chapters → human edit → format → publish**.

In Part Two, you will see that my first two book ideas, a Phnom Penh travel guide and a guide for expatriates, failed the demand check. That failure is part of the lesson.

It shows how AI can help you create and sell knowledge, as long as a human remains in charge of quality and truth.

### What the four have in common

None of these projects required me to become a programmer. Each began with a **problem I could describe in plain words**. Each was built in conversation with an AI agent: I explained, it built, I tested, it fixed. And each one taught me where AI is brilliant, and where it makes mistakes that only a human will catch.

That, I believe, is the skill everyone will need in the years ahead, in Cambodia and everywhere else. It is not about knowing how to code. It is about **knowing how to work with AI**.

In Part Two, I will show you the tools I use, what they cost, how to talk to AI so that it builds what you actually need, and then each project, step by step.
