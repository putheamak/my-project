# Part Three: What Comes Next

## Chapter 12: What I Learned the Hard Way

The four projects in this book taught me many individual lessons. Looking back, almost all of them gather into four larger truths. None of them is technical, and each applies far beyond my own work.

### When AI gets it wrong

AI made mistakes in every one of my projects. What surprised me was not that it erred, but how *quietly* it did so.

It wrote attractive promises into advertisements that my business had never agreed to make. It produced videos in which small symbols turned into empty boxes, without registering that anything was amiss. Even the checker it built for my blog had a blind spot, accepting one way of writing a phrase while rejecting another. None of these mistakes announced itself. Each came to light only because a person examined the finished work with care.

Writing this book in partnership with an AI produced its own collection of errors. I record them here because they are typical, and because each one carries a lesson.

**It stated facts it had not verified.** In an early plan, the AI asserted from memory that Khmer was not a supported language for Kindle books. To its credit, it flagged the claim as unconfirmed, but the episode was a reminder that an AI can sound entirely certain about something it has never checked.

**It contradicted information it already held.** An early draft of Chapter 5 stated that none of my projects had used paid advertising, although my own project notes recorded that Khmer Parcel had run paid Facebook advertisements. The sentence sounded perfectly reasonable. It was simply untrue.

**It made small errors that would have caused real confusion.** When building my weekly tracking sheet, it set the first "Monday" as the 20th of October, 2026, which was in fact a Tuesday. It caught the mistake itself while checking the calendar, but only because checking had become part of our routine.

**It embellished quotations beyond what the record supports.** One draft had Steve Jobs "telling a conference" that focusing is about saying no. On checking the sources, the AI found that he had said it while answering developers' questions, not in a prepared speech. In another case, it discovered that a famous line usually credited to the inventor Charles Kettering cannot be traced to anything he actually wrote.

**It guessed at my meaning.** When I said that my video advertisements took "around 30 to 60 seconds", it assumed I meant the length of each video, when I might have meant the time needed to make one. It wrote its interpretation into the draft and flagged it for me to confirm. That was the right behaviour, but only because I had asked it to mark every assumption.

The most reassuring moment came from the opposite direction. When I first asked an AI to write a program for my survey analysis, I did not simply accept its output. I compared its results, figure by figure, with those I had calculated by hand. Only when the two matched did I begin to rely on it.

That habit underpins everything else in this book. **Let AI do the work, but never let it be the only one checking that work.** Read every draft, watch every video, test every booking, and compare every important figure with a calculation you trust.

### Insecurity in the age of AI

One saying has travelled widely in recent years: "Hard times create strong men. Strong men create good times. Good times create weak men. And weak men create hard times."¹ It comes from a 2016 novel by the American writer G. Michael Hopf, and it echoes a far older observation by the fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun, who described how dynasties weakened over generations as hardship gave way to comfort. Historians rightly caution that real history is more complex than any four-line cycle. Yet the warning at its heart deserves attention, and I would express it this way: hard times create strong people, and comfortable times can make us careless.

The more comfortable we become, the weaker our foundations may grow. And AI is, above all, a technology of comfort.

AI will make possible many things that once seemed impossible; it already allows one person to accomplish what once required a team. Yet that very convenience creates a new kind of insecurity, and it is worth understanding why.

**More tools mean more doors.** Every new AI tool we adopt asks for access: to our email, our files, our customers and our accounts. Each connection makes us more productive, and each is also a door through which personal, public and private information can slip beyond our control, often through automated systems that no person is watching.

**Convenience is our default.** Human nature rarely chooses the difficult path when an easier one is available. When a tool offers to remember everything, upload everything and connect everything, most of us accept without reading the conditions. In this sense, AI and cybersecurity pull in opposite directions: one rewards us for sharing more, the other protects us by sharing less.

**The rules are still being written, bloc by bloc.** As AI spreads, governments and regional blocs are drafting new laws to govern it, and they are not drafting the same rules. The European Union has adopted its AI Act, the first comprehensive law of its kind. The United States, China and others are following their own, different paths, and in Southeast Asia the ASEAN member states have agreed a shared guide to AI governance and ethics.⁴ For small businesses and ordinary users in countries such as Cambodia, this means that the rules protecting our data may depend on where a service is based rather than where we live. What is permitted today may not be tomorrow, and much of what happens to our data takes place far beyond our view.

None of this is a reason to avoid AI. It is a reason to **keep learning**, so that our knowledge grows as quickly as our tools. The people most at risk are not those who use AI, but those who use it without understanding what they are giving away.

Fortunately, learning has never been more accessible. Several AI companies and educational organisations offer free, beginner-friendly courses that explain how AI works and how to use it responsibly:²

- **Anthropic Academy** (from the makers of Claude): free courses including *AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations* and *Claude 101*
- **OpenAI Academy** (from the makers of ChatGPT): free online lessons and events on using AI effectively
- **Google Cloud Skills Boost**: a free *Introduction to Generative AI* learning path
- **Microsoft Learn**: free training modules on the fundamentals of AI
- **IBM SkillsBuild**: free courses on AI and related digital skills
- **DeepLearning.AI**: *AI for Everyone*, a non-technical introduction by Andrew Ng, free to audit
- **Elements of AI**: a free online course created by the University of Helsinki and MinnaLearn

Choose one and begin. An hour of learning each week is a small price for the confidence to use these tools safely.

**The principle:** comfort is the hidden cost of convenience. Keep learning, keep questioning what you share, and never let an easy tool turn you into a careless user.

### Keeping your business, and other people, safe

The most serious risks I encountered had little to do with technology failing. They concerned trust being broken.

Some risks concern **your own business**. A bot's secret key is the key to your shop; it must never be pasted into a chat or shared with anyone. Any tool you place on the internet can be misused, so it is worth asking every time: *how could someone abuse this, and how do we prevent it?*

Other risks concern **other people**. Customers, survey respondents and entire communities entrust us with their information. As I described in Chapter 7, I built my first analysis tool using test data of my own and kept the real data offline. Convenience is never sufficient reason to expose what is not ours to give.

And some risks concern **honesty itself**: praising products I have not used, claiming results I have not achieved, making promises my business cannot keep, or entering a false country to gain access to an advertising platform. Each shortcut might bring a small advantage today. Each could cost a reputation that took years to build.

**Protect your secrets, protect other people's data, and protect your word.** In the age of AI, trust is the most valuable asset a small business possesses.

### The real cost is time

In Chapter 5, I showed that the tools behind all four projects cost about $31 a month. That figure is accurate, yet it misleads if read on its own. The true investment was **time**: hours spent describing problems, reviewing drafts, testing tools, correcting mistakes and learning how each system worked. ⭐ [Add your real hours from the weekly tracker: time spent building, and time saved each week.]

The return on that time is not always immediate. The booking app has only just been launched, the blog has yet to record its first sale, and several videos in the calendar are still to be made. Yet every hour has also bought something lasting: skills I did not have a few months ago, and tools that will continue working long after the hours spent building them.

**Count your hours as carefully as your dollars.** Then ask yourself honestly whether each project saves more time than it consumes, or teaches you something worth the investment.

### Working with AI in your own language

English is not my first language, and for many years that alone would have prevented me from building a website, writing a blog or publishing a book. AI has lowered that barrier dramatically. I write in simple, imperfect English, sometimes mixed with Khmer, and I am understood. My customers, in turn, receive messages, forms and videos in Khmer first, because that is the language in which they think and decide.

Yet here, too, there is a boundary. Some communities rightly reject text written by AI, and readers can often sense when words are not truly yours. Wherever your own voice matters, in a personal answer, a review or the story of your life, use AI to help you think, not to speak in your place.

**You do not need perfect language to build with AI. You need clear intentions, and the courage to keep your own voice.**

## Chapter 13: Your Turn

### Begin with a single step

The Chinese philosopher Laozi wrote that "a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step."³ Every project in this book began in exactly that way: with one problem, described in plain words, on an ordinary day.

You do not need a grand plan, a technical team or a large budget. You need one real problem, a few hours each day and the willingness to learn from your mistakes. The plan that follows will help you take that first step within a single week.

### A seven-day plan

**Day 1: Choose one problem.** Write down three tasks in your work that are repetitive, slow or prone to error, such as answering the same question, retyping the same information or producing the same kind of document. Choose the one that costs you or your customers the most time.

**Day 2: Write your handover note.** Describe the problem as your customer experiences it. Add the facts that must never be wrong: your prices, your contact details and your rules. List the promises you are not prepared to make. This note is your experience, ready for the AI to use.

**Day 3: Build the smallest useful version.** Share your note with the AI and ask what it would build, and why. Request the simplest possible version first, and follow the cycle from Chapter 7: describe, build, test, improve.

**Day 4: Test it with one real person.** Ask a customer, colleague or friend to use what you have built. Notice where they hesitate and listen to what confuses them. Do not explain or defend; simply observe.

**Day 5: Improve one thing at a time.** Return to the AI with what you have learned, and make one change at a time, testing after each.

**Day 6: Make it safe and honest.** Check where your secrets are stored, confirm that no real customer data has been shared unnecessarily, and read every word your tool shows to customers, removing any claim you cannot support.

**Day 7: Measure, and decide.** Record what has changed: time saved, mistakes avoided, customers' reactions. Then decide whether to keep improving this tool or to begin again with the next problem on your list.

By the end of the week, you will hold something more valuable than a finished product. You will have completed the entire cycle once, and you will know that you can do it again.

### A word to readers in Cambodia

I wrote this book in Phnom Penh, and I would like to close with a few words for readers in my own country, and in countries like it.

In Chapter 3, I described a new divide: between those who understand and use AI, and those who are left behind by it. That divide is not fixed. It is decided, person by person, by those who choose to learn. Many of us did not grow up with computers, and many still find technology intimidating. Yet the tools in this book require no degree in computer science. They require curiosity, patience and the courage to begin.

If you learn these skills, share them: with your family, your colleagues, your community and the young people around you. Every person who learns to work with AI makes the divide a little narrower. ⭐ [Optional: add a personal message, hope or call to action of your own.]

### A closing thought

When I began this book, I asked a question prompted by Jacob Coxon's warning: *if the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?*

I still cannot answer that question for the world. I can, however, answer it for myself. I am more ready than I was a few months ago, not because I understand everything about AI, but because I have used it, questioned it, corrected it and built real things with it. I have also learned where its limits lie, and where my own judgement must take over.

That is all I hope this book has given you: not a promise of easy income, but the confidence to begin, the habits to remain safe and honest, and the knowledge that anyone with a real problem and a clear explanation can build far more than they ever imagined.

The rest of the journey is yours.
