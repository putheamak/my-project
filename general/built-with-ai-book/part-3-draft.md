# Part Three: What Comes Next

---

## Chapter 12: What I Learned the Hard Way

The four projects taught me many small lessons. Looking back, most of them come down to four bigger ones. None of them is technical, and they apply far beyond my own work.

### When AI gets it wrong

AI made mistakes in every one of my projects. What surprised me was not that it made mistakes, but how *quietly* it made them.

It wrote promises my business had never agreed to make, and produced videos with broken symbols without noticing (both in Chapter 10). Even the checker it built for my blog had a blind spot: it accepted one way of writing a phrase and rejected another. None of these mistakes announced itself. I found each one only because I checked the finished work carefully.

Writing this book with an AI brought its own mistakes. I list them here because they are typical, and each one teaches something.

**It stated facts it had not verified.** In an early plan, the AI said from memory that Kindle did not support Khmer. To be fair, it marked this as unconfirmed. But it reminded me that an AI can sound completely sure about something it has never checked.

**It contradicted information it already held.** An early draft of Chapter 5 stated that none of my projects had used paid advertising, although my own project notes recorded that Khmer Parcel had run paid Facebook advertisements. The sentence sounded fine. It was simply wrong.

**It made small errors that could cause real confusion.** In my weekly tracking sheet, it set the first "Monday" as 20 October 2026, which was actually a Tuesday. It caught the mistake itself, but only because checking had become part of our routine.

**It made quotations sound bigger than the facts.** One draft had Steve Jobs "telling a conference" that focusing is about saying no. When it checked the sources, the AI found he said it while answering developers' questions, not in a speech. In another case, it found that a famous line usually credited to the inventor Charles Kettering cannot be traced to anything he wrote.

**It guessed at my meaning.** When I said that my video advertisements took "around 30 to 60 seconds", it assumed I meant the length of each video, when I actually meant [the time needed to make one / confirm]. It wrote its guess into the draft and marked it for me to confirm. That was right, but only because I had asked it to mark every guess.

The opposite also happened. When I first asked an AI to write a program for my survey analysis, I did not just accept the result. I compared it, number by number, with what I had calculated by hand. Only when they matched did I start to trust it.

That habit is behind everything else in this book. **Let AI do the work, but never let it be the only one checking that work.** Read every draft, watch every video, test every booking, and compare every important figure with a calculation you trust.

### Insecurity in the age of AI

One saying has spread widely in recent years: "Hard times create strong men. Strong men create good times. Good times create weak men. And weak men create hard times."[]{#ref-12-1}^[1](#note-12-1)^ It comes from a 2016 novel by the American writer G. Michael Hopf, and it echoes a far older observation by the fourteenth-century historian Ibn Khaldun, who described how dynasties weakened over generations as hardship gave way to comfort. Historians are right that real history is more complex than a four-line cycle. But the warning is worth hearing. I would say it this way: hard times create strong people, and comfortable times can make us careless.

The more comfortable we become, the weaker our foundations may grow. And AI is, above all, a technology of comfort.

AI will make many impossible things possible. It already lets one person do what once needed a team. But that same convenience creates a new kind of insecurity. Here is why.

**More tools mean more doors.** Every new AI tool asks for access: to our email, our files, our customers and our accounts. Each connection makes us more productive. Each one is also a door through which private information can slip out of our control, often through automatic systems that nobody is watching.

**Convenience is our default.** People rarely choose the hard way when there is an easy one. When a tool offers to remember, upload and connect everything, most of us say yes without reading the conditions. So AI and cybersecurity pull in opposite directions: one rewards us for sharing more, the other protects us by sharing less.

**The rules are still being written, bloc by bloc.** As AI spreads, governments and regional blocs are writing new laws for it, and they are not writing the same rules. The European Union has adopted its AI Act, the first comprehensive law of its kind. The United States, China and others are following their own, different paths, and in Southeast Asia the ASEAN member states have agreed a shared guide to AI governance and ethics.[]{#ref-12-2}^[2](#note-12-2)^ For small businesses and ordinary users in countries like Cambodia, this means the rules that protect our data may depend on where a service is based, not where we live. What is allowed today may not be allowed tomorrow, and much of what happens to our data happens where we cannot see it.

None of this is a reason to avoid AI. It is a reason to **keep learning**, so that our knowledge grows as quickly as our tools. The people most at risk are not those who use AI, but those who use it without knowing what they are giving away.

The good news: learning has never been easier. Several AI companies and schools offer free courses for beginners on how AI works and how to use it responsibly:[]{#ref-12-3}^[3](#note-12-3)^

- **Anthropic Academy** (from the makers of Claude): free courses including *AI Fluency: Framework & Foundations* and *Claude 101*
- **OpenAI Academy** (from the makers of ChatGPT): free online lessons and events on using AI effectively
- **Google Cloud Skills Boost**: a free *Introduction to Generative AI* learning path
- **Microsoft Learn**: free training modules on the fundamentals of AI
- **IBM SkillsBuild**: free courses on AI and related digital skills
- **DeepLearning.AI**: *AI for Everyone*, a non-technical introduction by Andrew Ng, free to audit
- **Elements of AI**: a free online course created by the University of Helsinki and MinnaLearn

Choose one and begin. One hour a week is a small price for the confidence to use these tools safely. Keep learning, keep asking what you share, and don't let an easy tool make you a careless user.

### Keeping your business, and other people, safe

The most serious risks I met were not about technology failing. They were about trust being broken.

Some risks concern **your own business**. A bot's secret key is the key to your shop; it must never be pasted into a chat or shared with anyone. Any tool you place on the internet can be misused, so it is worth asking every time: *how could someone abuse this, and how do we prevent it?*

Other risks concern **other people**. Customers, survey respondents and whole communities trust us with their information. As I described in Chapter 7, I built my first analysis tool using test data of my own and kept the real data offline. Convenience is never a good enough reason to expose what is not ours to give.

And some risks concern **honesty itself**: praising products I have not used, claiming results I have not achieved, making promises my business cannot keep, or entering a false country to gain access to an advertising platform (Chapter 9). Each shortcut might help a little today. Each one could cost a reputation that took years to build.

**Protect your secrets, protect other people's data, and protect your word.** In the age of AI, trust is the most valuable thing a small business has.

### The real cost is time

In Chapter 5, I showed that the tools behind all four projects cost about $31 a month. That number is true, but on its own it misleads. The real cost was **time**: hours of explaining problems, reviewing drafts, testing tools, fixing mistakes and learning how each system works. ⭐ [Add your real hours from the weekly tracker: time spent building, and time saved each week.]

That time does not always pay back quickly. The booking app has only just launched, the blog has no sale yet, and most videos in the calendar are still to be made. But every hour also bought something that lasts: skills I did not have a few months ago, and tools that keep working long after I built them.

Count your hours as carefully as your dollars. Then ask yourself honestly: does this project save more time than it takes, or teach me something worth the time?

### Working with AI in your own language

English is not my first language. For many years, that alone would have stopped me from building a website, writing a blog or publishing a book. AI has made that barrier much lower. I write in simple, imperfect English, sometimes mixed with Khmer, and I am understood. My customers, in turn, receive messages, forms and videos in Khmer first, because that is the language in which they think and decide.

But here too there is a limit. Some communities rightly reject text written by AI, and readers can often feel when the words are not really yours. Wherever your own voice matters, in a personal answer, a review or the story of your life, use AI to help you think, not to speak in your place.

**You do not need perfect language to build with AI. You need clear intentions, and the courage to keep your own voice.**

## Chapter 13: Your Turn

### Begin with a single step

The Chinese philosopher Laozi wrote that "a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step."[]{#ref-13-1}^[1](#note-13-1)^ Every project in this book started that way: one problem, explained in plain words, on an ordinary day.

You don't need a big plan, a technical team or a large budget. You need one real problem, a few hours a day and the willingness to learn from your mistakes. The plan below helps you take that first step in one week.

### A seven-day plan

**Day 1: Choose one problem.** Write down three tasks in your work that are repetitive, slow or easy to get wrong, such as answering the same question, retyping the same information or producing the same kind of document. Choose the one that costs you or your customers the most time.

**Day 2: Write your handover note.** Describe the problem as your customer experiences it. Add the facts that must never be wrong: your prices, your contact details and your rules. List the promises you are not ready to make. This note is your experience, ready for the AI to use.

**Day 3: Build the smallest useful version.** Share your note with the AI and ask what it would build, and why. Ask for the simplest version first, and follow the cycle from Chapter 7: describe, build, test, improve.

**Day 4: Test it with one real person.** Ask a customer, colleague or friend to use what you have built. Notice where they hesitate and listen to what confuses them. Don't explain or defend. Just watch.

**Day 5: Improve one thing at a time.** Return to the AI with what you have learned, and make one change at a time, testing after each.

**Day 6: Make it safe and honest.** Check where your secrets are stored, confirm that no real customer data has been shared unnecessarily, and read every word your tool shows to customers, removing any claim you cannot support.

**Day 7: Measure, and decide.** Record what has changed: time saved, mistakes avoided, customers' reactions. Then decide whether to keep improving this tool or to begin again with the next problem on your list.

By the end of the week, you will have something more valuable than a finished product. You will have done the whole cycle once, and you will know you can do it again.

### A word to readers in Cambodia

I wrote this book in Phnom Penh, and I want to end with a few words for readers in my own country, and in countries like it.

In Chapter 3, I described a new divide: between those who understand and use AI, and those who are left behind by it. Living here, I see that divide every day, and it has several layers.

**The skills divide.** Many of us use smartphones, Facebook and Telegram every day. We pay with QR codes and sell online. Yet using an app is not the same as understanding the technology behind it. Few people have learned yet to give AI a clear task, check its work and protect their data at the same time. That gap, not access to the internet, is the new divide.

**The language divide.** Most AI tools, courses and guides are written in English. That leaves out many capable people who think and work in Khmer. AI is improving in our language, and it already understands mixed Khmer and English far better than it did a few years ago. But the best learning materials still assume English, and that slows us down.

**The work divide.** Many of our jobs, in garment factories, in offices and in services, are exactly the kinds of work that automation and AI may change first. Our young population can be our greatest strength in the age of AI, but only if young people gain the skills that machines cannot easily replace: understanding customers, solving real problems and directing intelligent tools.

**The safety divide.** Where digital literacy is still growing, people are more exposed to scams, fake news and careless sharing of personal data. As I wrote in Chapter 7, convenience can quietly become a risk when the rules are not clear.

None of these divides is fixed. Each one closes, person by person, when people choose to learn. Many of us did not grow up with computers, and many still find technology scary. But the tools in this book need no computer science degree. They need curiosity, patience and the courage to begin.

So here is what I ask of you.

If you are a **student**, learn to use AI as a partner for thinking, not as a shortcut for homework. The habit of checking its work will help you all your life.

If you are a **worker**, look at your own job and find one repetitive task that AI could help with. Learning that one skill may protect your future more than any certificate.

If you **run a business**, start with one real problem, as I did. You do not need a large budget; you need a clear explanation and the patience to test.

If you are a **teacher, a trainer or a leader** in an NGO or community, share what you learn in Khmer. Every guide, workshop or conversation in our own language narrows the divide for many others.

If you learn these skills, share them: with your family, your colleagues, your community and the young people around you. Every person who learns to work with AI makes the divide a little narrower, and together we can make sure that Cambodia does not watch this change from the outside, but takes part in it.

### A closing thought

When I started this book, I asked a question after reading Jacob Coxon's warning: *if the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?*

I still cannot answer that question for the world. But I can answer it for myself. I am more ready than I was a few months ago, not because I understand everything about AI, but because I have used it, questioned it, corrected it and built real things with it. I have also learned where its limits are, and where my own judgement must take over.

That is all I hope this book gives you: not a promise of easy income, but the confidence to begin, the habits to stay safe and honest, and the knowledge that anyone with a real problem and a clear explanation can build much more than they imagined.

The rest of the journey is yours.

## Glossary

Plain-language explanations of the technical terms used in this book.

**Affiliate marketing.** Recommending products, usually through articles or videos, and earning a small commission when a reader buys through your special link.

**AI agent.** An AI that does not only answer questions but carries out tasks, such as writing files, running programs and fixing its own mistakes. Claude Code is an AI agent.

**Amazon Associates.** Amazon's affiliate programme. Members earn a commission on qualifying purchases made through their links.

**API (Application Programming Interface).** A way for one piece of software to talk to another automatically. My blog engine uses the Claude API to request drafts without anyone typing in a chat window.

**API key.** A secret password that allows your software to use an API. Anyone who has it can use your account, so it must never be shared or pasted into a chat.

**Best Sellers Rank (BSR).** A number Amazon shows on each product page. The lower the number, the more the product is selling compared with others in its category.

**Bot.** A small automated account, for example on Telegram, that can reply to messages, show buttons and open apps.

**Build journal.** A record of how a project was built, what was decided, what went wrong and what happened next. This book is one.

**Claude / Claude Code.** Claude is the AI assistant made by Anthropic. Claude Code is the version that works as an agent, reading and writing project files to build tools.

**Cloudflare Workers.** A service that runs small apps on Cloudflare's network, so you do not need to rent or maintain your own server. Its free plan is enough for a small business.

**COD (cash on delivery).** A payment method in which the customer pays the delivery rider when the parcel arrives.

**Compliance check.** An automatic review of a draft against a set of rules, such as Amazon's affiliate rules, before anything is published.

**Domain name.** The address people type to find a website, such as *homepicksunder30.com*.

**Emoji.** Small picture symbols used in messages. Some computers cannot display them, which is why they appeared as empty boxes in my early videos.

**GitHub.** An online service that stores project files, keeps a history of every change, and can run small jobs on a schedule.

**GitHub Pages.** A free GitHub feature that publishes a simple website directly from your project files.

**GoatCounter.** A simple, privacy-friendly service that counts how many people visit a website.

**Handover note.** A short document that tells an AI, or a new colleague, the facts and rules of a project, so they can start work without asking the same questions again.

**HTML.** The language web pages are written in. My calculator and my video scenes are built from HTML.

**KDP (Kindle Direct Publishing).** Amazon's service for publishing ebooks and paperbacks yourself.

**KHQR.** Cambodia's national QR code standard for digital payments.

**Kindle Unlimited.** A subscription that lets readers borrow many Kindle books. Authors are paid according to the number of pages read.

**KV (key-value storage).** A small, simple database. My booking app uses Cloudflare's free KV storage to remember which sellers have used the launch offer.

**Mini app.** A small app that opens inside another app, such as Telegram, with nothing to install.

**Net Promoter Score (NPS).** A common measure of how likely customers are to recommend a brand to others.

**Prompt.** The message or instruction you give to an AI.

**Python.** A popular programming language, often used for data analysis. My first AI-written program was in Python.

**Reels.** Short vertical videos on Facebook and Instagram, similar to TikTok videos.

**Repository (repo).** A project folder stored on GitHub, together with the history of every change.

**Render.** To produce a finished image or video from a design. My video factory renders each scene frame by frame.

**SEO (search engine optimisation).** Ways of writing and organising web pages so that search engines such as Google can find and rank them.

**Secret.** A password or key, such as a bot token or API key, stored in a protected place on GitHub or Cloudflare so a project can use it without anyone being able to read it.

**Server.** A computer that is always switched on and connected to the internet, running an app or a website.

**SPSS.** Software widely used for statistical analysis in research.

**Superintelligence.** A possible future AI that is more capable than humans in almost every field.

**Terminal.** A text window for typing commands to a computer. Programmers use it often; beginners can start with Claude Code in a web browser instead.

**Test data.** Invented data in the same format as real data, used to build or test a tool without exposing anyone's private information.

**Token (bot token).** The secret key Telegram gives you when you create a bot. Whoever holds it controls the bot.
