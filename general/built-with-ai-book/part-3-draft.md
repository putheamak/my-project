# Part Three: What Comes Next

## Chapter 12: What I Learned the Hard Way

The four projects in this book taught me many separate lessons. Looking back, most of them fall into four larger truths. None is technical, and every one of them applies far beyond my own work.

### When AI gets it wrong

AI made mistakes in every project. That surprised me less than how *quietly* it made them.

It wrote attractive promises into advertisements that my business had never agreed to make. It produced videos in which small symbols turned into empty boxes, without noticing anything was wrong. Even the checker it built for my blog had a blind spot, accepting one way of writing a phrase while rejecting another. None of these mistakes announced itself. Each was found only because a person looked carefully at the finished work.

The most reassuring moment came from the opposite direction. When I first asked an AI to write a program for my survey analysis, I did not simply trust its results. I compared them, line by line, with the figures I had produced by hand. Only when the two matched did I begin to rely on it.

That habit is the foundation of everything else in this book. **Let AI do the work; never let it be the only one checking the work.** Read every draft, watch every video, test every booking, and compare every important number with a calculation you trust.

### Keeping your business, and other people, safe

The most serious risks I met were not about technology failing. They were about trust being broken.

Some risks concern **your own business**. A bot's secret key is the key to your shop, so it must never be pasted into a chat or shared with anyone. Any tool you place on the internet can be misused, so it is worth asking, every time: *how could someone abuse this, and how do we prevent it?*

Other risks concern **other people**. Customers, survey respondents and communities trust us with their information. As I explained in Chapter 7, I built my first analysis tool with test data of my own and kept the real data offline. Convenience is never a good enough reason to expose what is not ours to give.

And some risks concern **honesty itself**: products I have not used, results I have not achieved, promises my business cannot keep, or a false country entered to gain access to an advertising platform. Each shortcut might bring a small gain today. Each could cost a reputation that took years to build.

**Protect your secrets, protect other people's data, and protect your word.** In the age of AI, trust is the most valuable thing a small business owns.

### The real cost is time

In Chapter 5, I showed that the tools behind all four projects cost about $31 a month. That figure is accurate, and it is also misleading if read alone. The real investment was **time**: hours spent describing problems, reviewing drafts, testing tools, correcting mistakes and learning how each system worked. ⭐ [Add your real hours from the weekly tracker: time spent building, and time saved each week.]

The return on that time is not always immediate. The booking app has only just been launched. The blog has yet to make a sale. Some of the videos in the calendar are still waiting to be made. Yet every hour has also bought something lasting: skills I did not have a few months ago, and tools that will keep working long after the hours spent building them.

**Count your hours as carefully as your dollars.** Then ask, honestly, whether each project is saving more time than it consumes, or teaching you something worth the investment.

### Working with AI in your own language

English is not my first language, and for years that alone would have stopped me from building a website, writing a blog or publishing a book. AI has lowered that barrier dramatically. I write in simple, imperfect English, sometimes mixed with Khmer, and I am understood. My customers receive messages, forms and videos in Khmer first, because that is the language in which they think and decide.

Yet there is a boundary here too. Some communities rightly reject text written by AI, and readers can often sense when words are not truly yours. Where your own voice matters, in a personal answer, a review or the story of your life, use AI to help you think, not to speak in your place.

**You do not need perfect language to build with AI. You need clear intentions, and the courage to keep your own voice.**

## Chapter 13: Your Turn

### Begin with one step

The Chinese philosopher Laozi wrote that "a journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step."¹ Every project in this book began exactly that way: with one problem, described in plain words, on an ordinary day.

You do not need a grand plan, a technical team or a large budget. You need one real problem, a few hours each day, and the willingness to learn from your mistakes. The plan below will help you take your first step in a single week.

### A seven-day plan

**Day 1 — Choose one problem.** Write down three tasks in your work that are repetitive, slow or error-prone: answering the same question, retyping the same information, producing the same kind of document. Choose the one that costs you or your customers the most time.

**Day 2 — Write your handover note.** Describe the problem as your customer experiences it. Add the facts that must never be wrong: your prices, your contact details, your rules. Add the promises you are not willing to make. This note is your experience, ready for the AI to use.

**Day 3 — Build the smallest useful version.** Share your note with the AI and ask what it would build, and why. Ask for the simplest possible version first. Remember the cycle from Chapter 7: describe, build, test, improve.

**Day 4 — Test it with one real person.** Ask one customer, colleague or friend to use what you have built. Watch where they hesitate and listen to what confuses them. Do not explain or defend; simply observe.

**Day 5 — Improve one thing at a time.** Return to the AI with what you learned, and make one change at a time, testing after each.

**Day 6 — Make it safe and honest.** Check where your secrets are kept. Make sure no real customer data has been shared without need. Read every word your tool shows to customers and remove any claim you cannot support.

**Day 7 — Measure, and decide.** Write down what has changed: time saved, mistakes avoided, customer reactions. Then decide whether to keep improving this tool, or to begin again with the next problem on your list.

At the end of the week, you will have something more valuable than a finished product. You will have completed the whole cycle once, and you will know that you can do it again.

### A word to readers in Cambodia

I wrote this book in Phnom Penh, and I want to end with a few words for readers in my own country, and in other countries like it.

In Chapter 3, I described a new divide: between people who understand and use AI, and those who are left behind by it. That divide is not fixed. It is decided, person by person, by who chooses to learn. Many of us did not grow up with computers, and many of us still find technology intimidating. But the tools in this book require no degree in computer science. They require curiosity, patience and the courage to try.

If you learn these skills, share them: with your family, your colleagues, your community and the young people around you. Every person who learns to work with AI makes the divide a little narrower. ⭐ [Optional: add a personal message, hope or call to action of your own.]

### A closing thought

When I began this book, I asked a question inspired by Jacob Coxon's warning: *if the people who build AI are this worried, are the rest of us ready?*

I still cannot answer that question for the world. But I can answer it for myself. I am more ready than I was a few months ago, not because I understand everything about AI, but because I have used it, questioned it, corrected it and built real things with it. I have also learned where its limits lie, and where my own judgement must take over.

That is all I hope this book has given you: not a promise of easy income, but the confidence to begin, the habits to stay safe and honest, and the knowledge that a person with a real problem and a clear explanation can build far more than they ever imagined.

The rest of the journey is yours.
