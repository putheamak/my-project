# Editorial Review: *Built with AI, No Coding* by Mak Puthea

Reviewer: independent non-fiction editor
Files reviewed: `manuscript.md` (about 23,500 words) and `amazon-description.md`
Date of review: 27 October 2026

---

## 1. Overall verdict

The book is **not ready to publish yet, but it is close.** With four to six weeks of focused work it can be a good, honest book that stands out in a crowded market. Its **biggest strength is honesty**: real costs, real numbers (even when they are zero), checked quotations and an open account of how AI helped write it. Almost no book in this category does this. Its **biggest weakness is that the book does not yet deliver what the title promises fast enough or fully enough.** The reader waits about 5,500 words (Introduction and Part One) before any building starts. Part One drifts into general essays on the future of AI, war and basic income. And the four projects are told as stories, but a true beginner could not rebuild them from the Builder's Notes alone.

## 3. Top 10 issues (most important first)

### 1. Part One is too long and partly off-promise
- **Where:** Chapters 2 and 3 (about 2,100 words), e.g. "In the Middle East, the war that followed the US and Israeli strikes on Iran in early 2026 has spread…" and the five-point plan "If superintelligence comes and there are not enough jobs".
- **Problem:** The title and description promise real tools, real costs and real mistakes. A small-business reader in the US meets four chapters about corporate politics, AI timelines, geopolitics, universal basic income and Cambodian social policy before any building starts. Many Kindle readers use "Look Inside" and the free sample. Right now the sample ends before the book's best material begins.
- **Fix:** Merge Chapters 2 and 3 into one chapter of about 1,200–1,500 words called "Why This Matters Now". Keep the Coxon hook, the "new divide" idea and the story of the unconnected company systems. Cut the war paragraph completely. Move the policy ideas (UBI, robot tax, national AI fund) to a short optional final essay, or remove them. Shorten Chapter 1 by about a third (see issue 6). The aim is for building to start by about 12–15% of the book.

### 2. The results are missing, and some text will be out of date on launch day
- **Where:** Chapter 9 ("The number of sales was zero. ⭐"), Chapter 10 (⭐ on videos made), Chapter 12 (⭐ on hours spent), Chapter 8 ("I intend to share them honestly in a future edition"), Chapter 11 ("At the time of writing, this book is not yet finished. Ahead lie the final chapters…").
- **Problem:** The book's promise is "real results", but every project ends with "it is too early to say". The booking app chapter has **no usage numbers and no placeholder for them**, even though it is the project most likely to have real data by December. The ⭐ in Chapter 12 (hours spent and hours saved) matters most, because "the real cost is time" is one of the book's main claims and it has no number behind it. Chapter 11 describes the book as unfinished, which will read strangely in a published book.
- **Fix:** Fill every ⭐ with data up to a fixed date (for example 30 November 2026). Add a short **"Status on [date]"** box at the end of each project chapter: bookings through the app, visitors, clicks, sales, videos posted, hours spent. Add booking-app numbers to Chapter 8. Rewrite the end of Chapter 11 in the past tense ("I finished the book in November…") and delete the ⭐ that asks for post-launch sales, because you cannot fill it before launch.

### 3. A true beginner cannot rebuild the projects from the Builder's Notes
- **Where:** Chapter 5 ("It also includes **Claude Code**, the AI agent that actually builds things"), Chapters 8–10 Builder's Notes.
- **Problem:** The book never tells the reader **how to start Claude Code**: where to open it (web, desktop app or terminal), how it connects to GitHub, what the approval prompts look like, or what a "repository" is. The video factory needs a computer that runs a browser and assembles frames, but the book never says where that happens. Chapter 9 says the engine "runs there three times a week" but never names GitHub Actions or explains it. Readers who try will get stuck in the first ten minutes, and that is exactly the kind of reader who leaves a 2-star review.
- **Fix:** Add a section to Chapter 5, or an appendix, called "Getting Set Up: Your First Hour with Claude Code", with 4–6 screenshots. Give each project a label such as "Difficulty: medium · Time: one weekend · You need: GitHub + Cloudflare accounts". Be honest that Projects 2 and 3 are harder than Project 1.

### 4. The voice is not yet the author's voice
- **Where:** Throughout. Examples: "without registering that anything was amiss" (Ch 12), "products people buy without lengthy deliberation" (Ch 9), "At first, I found that exhilarating" (Ch 6), and 8 sections ending in a bold "**Why it matters:**" line plus many bold one-line morals.
- **Problem:** The prose is correct but formal, British in tone and very regular in rhythm: "not X, but Y", a bold moral at the end of every section, "honest" (30 times), "real" (68 times), "plain" (40 times). Many readers now spot this as AI-polished text, and some Amazon reviewers punish it. It also works against your own advice in Chapter 12: "use AI to help you think, not to speak in your place." The best lines in the book are your own words: "very small demand", "if your belief in overcoming hard times stays strong, the light ahead will only grow brighter", "I was really surprised".
- **Fix:** Do one voice pass. Put back some of your own sentences from your notes, even if they are simpler. Add more concrete moments (what you saw on the screen, what a seller said, how you felt). Keep "Why it matters" in Chapter 6, where it works as a list, but remove most bold morals elsewhere. Use plainer words (see section 7).

### 5. The same stories appear three or four times
- **Where:** Emoji as empty boxes (Ch 6, 10, 12, glossary); the wrong digit on the poster (Ch 6, 10); same-day promises (Ch 6, 10, 12); the drop at the seventeenth second and the $5/day ads (Ch 6, 10); Pinterest refusing Cambodia (Ch 6, 9, 12); the failed travel guide (Ch 4, 6, 11); "imperfect English mixed with Khmer" (Intro, Ch 6, 11, 12). Chapter 4 also describes all four projects in detail before Part Two describes them again.
- **Problem:** Readers notice the repetition, and it makes the book feel padded and the experience feel thin ("is this the only mistake he made?").
- **Fix:** Tell each story **once, in full**, in its home chapter. Elsewhere, refer back in one short clause ("as with the emoji problem in Chapter 10"). In Chapter 6, use the stories as short pointers or swap in different examples. Cut Chapter 4 to about 400–500 words: a one-paragraph map of each project.

### 6. Legal and fairness risk: the former employer
- **Where:** Chapter 1: "New people were hired under conditions that, in my view, raised serious questions of ethics and professionalism. Some colleagues were pushed out." Also "politics comes before performance" and "New employees seemed to be judged less on their work than on whose side they were on".
- **Problem:** The company is not named, but "one of the leading consumer brands in its industry" in Cambodia, plus your three-year role and LinkedIn profile, makes it easy for local readers to identify. An accusation about ethics is the kind of statement that can lead to a complaint or a demand to remove the book, and it adds nothing to a book about building tools.
- **Fix:** Delete the "ethics and professionalism" sentence and the "Then came the cuts" paragraph. Describe your own experience ("I felt my research was not being used") rather than other people's motives. Keep the Grantham and Keynes ideas: they are the useful part. Consider making the employer less identifiable ("a large company in Cambodia").

### 7. Recent news about real people needs careful checking
- **Where:** Chapter 2 (Jacob Coxon: age 27, British, resigned 8 September 2026, the exact quote, "about 76 million views", the WSJ interview, "out of control by the end of 2027"); Chapter 3 (strikes on Iran "in early 2026", Houthi–Saudi truce collapse in July); Chapter 1, note 3 (the Grantham "bubble" claim sourced only to a news-aggregator site).
- **Problem:** These are the newest and least-checked claims in the book, and they are about real, named people. Coxon is also the frame for the whole book (Ch 2 and the closing thought in Ch 13). One wrong detail here would damage the "nothing invented" promise. Some URLs look unusual (the X handle "hilbertspaess" does not match the name; `time.com/article/2026/…`; `techland.time.com/?p=…`; `also.kottke.org/…`).
- **Fix:** Check every detail against the original sources yourself: the X post, the WSJ article, TIME, PBS, and the Diary of a CEO episode (with its date). Replace aggregator links with primary sources. Use "reportedly" for view counts. Cut the war paragraph (see issue 1).

### 8. The book's promise and its reality do not quite match
- **Where:** Introduction ("I built four working tools"), Chapter 4 ("An ebook machine"), Amazon description ("four real business tools… An ebook system"), but Chapter 11: "My original plan was an automated system… I never built it, and I no longer wish to." Also the opening line "I am not a programmer. I studied computer science…"
- **Problem:** A careful reader will feel misled: the fourth "tool" is really a way of working (a chat plus a few files). Separately, a US beginner may read "computer science graduate" and think "this is not for people like me." The title says "No Coding", yet the author has a CS degree and used AI-written Python at work.
- **Fix:** Call Project Four what it is, "a book-writing workflow", in the Introduction, Chapter 4, the chapter title and the description. Say it honestly: "three tools and one method". Address the degree head-on in two sentences: "My degree was in networking: cables and routers, not software. I had never written an app." Lead the Introduction with a scene, not the qualification (see Chapter notes).

### 9. Some platform rules are simplified or may be wrong
- **Where:** Chapter 9 ("you must not use Amazon's product images", "You must not display prices that may change, nor copy star ratings"); Chapter 5 (GitHub Pages hosting the affiliate blog for free; Claude Pro "includes Claude Code"); Chapter 9 (Cambodia payment by gift card only; Pinterest Ads not available; Reddit rule).
- **Problem:** Amazon Associates does allow product images, prices and ratings when they come through Amazon's official tools (SiteStripe or the Product Advertising API) and are kept up to date; what is not allowed is copying them by hand. GitHub's terms say Pages is not meant to host sites that are mainly commercial; an affiliate blog may be a grey area. Claude plan contents and usage limits change often, and heavy Claude Code use on the $20 plan may hit limits.
- **Fix:** Re-check each rule on the official pages in the week before upload, and reword to "copied by hand" where needed. Add one sentence on usage limits ("if you use Claude Code heavily, you may reach the Pro plan's limit and have to wait or upgrade"). Note in Chapter 9 that payment and advertising rules are "as of November 2026".

### 10. Kindle production problems: notes, contents, images, glossary
- **Where:** Notes sections; Contents; end of the book.
- **Problem:** The note numbers start again in each Part and are out of order in the text (Part Two: ⁵ appears before ⁴, and ⁸ before ⁷; Part Three: ⁴ before ² and ³). The Part Three notes come *after* "A Request to the Reader" and are not in the Contents. The book has no images except the author photo, yet it describes a mini app, a video ad and a blog that readers would love to see. The glossary defines "Kindle Unlimited", which the book never uses, but leaves out "Command Prompt", "repository", "GitHub Actions" and "webhook / setup address".
- **Fix:** Use per-chapter endnotes, numbered in reading order and linked both ways. Put all notes before the Glossary. Add 6–10 screenshots (the booking form, the booking arriving in the group, one ad frame, the blog home page, a compliance report, the Facebook ad results, the Amazon search results from Chapter 11). Update the glossary.

## 4. Chapter-by-chapter notes

### Front matter (title page, copyright, How This Book Was Written, Contents)
- Copyright, disclaimer, trademark, affiliate and privacy notes are thorough and professional. Good.
- "How This Book Was Written" is a strength: open and clear. But "helped to… verify facts and the sources of quotations" overclaims; an AI cannot fully verify facts. Change it to "helped me check facts and sources, which I then reviewed."
- "No results, quotations or experiences in this book have been invented" is a strong promise. Make sure issue 7 is fixed first, or this line becomes a liability.
- The Contents leaves out the three Notes sections. Make sure the subtitle on the title page matches the cover and the KDP metadata exactly.
- "programme member" → "program member" (US spelling).

### Introduction
- Clear, warm, well-organised; "A promise, and a confession" is the best part.
- Weak first lines: "I am not a programmer. I studied computer science…" raises a doubt in the first breath. Open with a scene instead, for example the first booking arriving in the office group, or the "AI + KDP = ATM" advertisement, then make the claim.
- "Who this book is for" and "It is probably not the right book if…" are excellent. Keep them.
- "Part Two… how you can build your own first small tool in about thirty minutes" is a strong hook; move it higher.
- The "How to read this book" section repeats the Part Two box. Keep one.

### Chapter 1: The Job That Taught Me What I Didn't Know
- The story of teaching yourself KoboToolbox → Excel → SPSS → Python with AI is good and relevant. Keep it.
- The Grantham "wrong together / wrong alone" idea is interesting, but the section is long (about 1,480 words) and only loosely tied to building tools. Cut it by a third.
- Remove the ethics accusation and the "cuts" paragraph (issue 6).
- "I was one of them." is unclear: one of the newcomers or one of the experienced staff? (see section 7).
- The ending ("This book is about that new version of me") works well.

### Chapter 2: A Warning from Inside the Labs
- The Coxon opening is a strong hook and gives the book its central question. Keep it, short and well sourced.
- "What is coming" and "What it could mean for daily life" are general opinion that readers can find anywhere; phrases like "most forecasts expect" and "almost everyone agrees" are unsupported. Cut heavily or say whose forecasts.
- The three-scenarios table is clear but not needed in a business-tools book.
- Merge with Chapter 3 (issue 1).

### Chapter 3: The New Divide
- The story of the company with unconnected systems is the best part: it is concrete and it is yours. Keep it.
- The garment-jobs "ladder" point is strong for developing-country readers; keep one paragraph.
- "A world already under pressure" (wars) dates fast, carries accuracy risk and is off-topic. Delete.
- The five-part policy plan and "Two possible endings" read like a policy paper. Move to an optional closing essay or cut.

### Chapter 4: Why I Built Four Things
- The rule "every project must solve a real problem; none can be a mere experiment" conflicts with the blog, which you describe as a test of a promise. Rephrase: "every project must solve a real problem or test a real question."
- It describes each project in almost the same detail as Part Two (the mini app paragraph, the video factory paragraph). Cut to a short map of about 400–500 words.
- "I am a believer: AI is coming" is vague; AI is already here. Say what you believe.
- "What the four have in common" is good; keep it as the bridge to Part Two.

### Chapter 5: The Toolkit, and What It Really Costs
- Very strong: the cost table and "a subscription and an API are not the same thing" are exactly what readers want.
- Add how to start Claude Code (issue 3) and a note about plan usage limits.
- "Everything… built with just four core tools" leaves out the Claude API ($10/month), which the table includes. Say "four core tools plus the API".
- "the price of a few cups of coffee each week": $31 a month is about $7 a week, one or two coffees in the US. Adjust.
- Add one sentence for readers who use ChatGPT or Gemini: can they follow along? Many buyers will ask.

### Chapter 6: How to Talk to AI So It Builds What You Need
- One of the best chapters. The young-engineer story is original and memorable, and the "two halves" idea is the heart of the book.
- The eight lessons are useful, and the prompt toolkit table is excellent: perhaps the most copied page in the book.
- Many lessons use stories that are told again later (issue 5). Shorten those examples here, or let the later chapters refer back.
- Lesson 4 talks about choosing products for the blog before the blog has been explained; this is confusing. Use a simpler example.
- "Large AI models are trained on vast quantities of human experience" is a loose comparison; "human writing" is more accurate.

### Chapter 7: Your First Small Win
- Excellent practical chapter; the 30-minute calculator is the right first project.
- "The line I would not cross" (test data) is an important ethical lesson, but it is long. The Cambodiana Hotel story and the Buffett quote could be shortened by half.
- The example price "$1, plus $0.25 for every kilometre after the first three" could be mistaken for Khmer Parcel's real price (Chapter 8 says 3,000 riel). Mark it clearly as an invented example.
- Add one screenshot of a finished calculator.

### Chapter 8: Project One — Telegram Booking App
- Well structured; the "Why…" headings explain decisions clearly. "Meet your customers where they already are" and "Build something you can maintain yourself" are strong lessons.
- Missing: real usage numbers since 1 October (how many bookings came through the app, how many sellers tried it). Add these, as this is your best chance at a real result.
- The launch offer (2,000 riel for the first three deliveries) will expire, while the book stays on sale for years. Write it in the past tense ("At launch, we offered…").
- The "habits are the hard part" section is honest and valuable; expand it with what you actually did to persuade sellers.
- Builder's Notes step 4 ("opening the setup address") will confuse beginners. Explain in one sentence what this does.

### Chapter 9: Project Two — The Blog
- The longest chapter (about 2,640 words). The honest results and "the production can be automated; the audience cannot" are the best ideas in the book. This could be the chapter that sells it.
- The section about global marketplaces, robots and drones ("I also see this work as a window into the future") is off-topic. Cut it.
- The human-centred scoring table is useful. But you recommend scoring "every post" without saying whether you do it yourself, or what your scores were. Show one real example.
- Check the Amazon Associates rules wording (issue 9) and whether GitHub Pages permits this use.
- Make sure the blog name and domain ("Home Picks Under 30", homepicksunder30.com) exactly match the live site at launch.

### Chapter 10: Project Three — Video Ads
- The ad results list (2,546 views, 605 three-second views, 4 seconds average, 57 clicks, 0 bookings, 4 rider applications) is the most convincing data in the book. Excellent.
- "Rules learned the hard way" repeats Chapter 6 almost word for word. Tell the stories fully here and cut them from Chapter 6.
- The Ogilvy quote ("she is your wife") may feel dated or sexist to some US readers today. Either explain the context in one line or choose another quote.
- Fill the ⭐ on videos made; this is your "video a day" promise.
- Explain in one sentence where the video factory runs (your computer? a cloud session?) and what "silent soundtrack" is for.
- Chapter 12 says the AI was unsure whether "30 to 60 seconds" meant video length or time to make one. Chapters 4 and 10 still say video length. Confirm which is true.

### Chapter 11: Project Four — The Ebook Machine and This Book
- The opening ("AI + KDP = ATM") is a great hook. Consider using it, or a short version, at the start of the Introduction.
- "Three ideas, two failures" is the most useful demand-research lesson in the book. Add screenshots.
- The MLK section is heartfelt, but comparing your early-stage projects with the darkest moment of the civil-rights struggle may feel out of proportion to some US readers. Keep your own line about belief and light. Shorten or remove the MLK paragraph.
- Rewrite "What remains to be done" in the past tense; it is time-sensitive (issue 2).
- Rename the "machine" honestly (issue 8).

### Chapter 12: What I Learned the Hard Way
- "When AI gets it wrong" is strong and specific; the list of AI errors while writing the book is unusual and credible.
- "Insecurity in the age of AI" is the weakest section. The Hopf "strong men" quote is known in some circles as a political meme, the link to AI is loose, and "bloc by bloc" is easy to misread. Cut the quote and keep the "more tools mean more doors" ideas.
- The free-course list is useful, but course names change quickly. Keep the note "as of [date]" and check every name.
- "The real cost is time" has no number. This is the most important ⭐ in the book (issue 2).
- "Keeping your business, and other people, safe" repeats Chapters 7 and 8. Shorten it to a checklist.

### Chapter 13: Your Turn
- The seven-day plan is clear and practical, a good ending. Consider a one-page printable checklist version.
- "a few hours each day" conflicts with "thirty minutes" in Chapter 7 and may scare busy owners. Say "about an hour a day".
- "A word to readers in Cambodia" is moving and important to you. But most buyers will be outside Cambodia. Retitle it "A word to readers in Cambodia and other developing countries", and shorten the four "divides", which repeat Chapter 3.
- The closing thought, which returns to the Coxon question, is a good ending. Keep it.

### Glossary
- Clear, short and useful. Good.
- Remove "Kindle Unlimited" (not used in the book), or mention it in Chapter 11.
- Add: repository, GitHub Actions, Command Prompt / terminal, webhook (or "setup address"), frame, riel, "hook" (in video ads).
- "Claude Code is the version that works as an agent" is fine, but say where you use it (web, app or terminal).

### About the Author
- Good, clear and in the third person. Mentioning qualitative research and NGOs adds warmth.
- The personal Gmail and mobile number will be public for years and may attract spam. Consider a dedicated book email (or a contact page on your website). Check that the Telegram contact is right: the business may use a different Telegram number from the phone number. Add www.khmerparcel.com.
- The photo path (`images/author-photo.jpg`) must exist when you build the ebook.
- "rather than be left behind" → "rather than being left behind".

### A Request to the Reader
- Polite, honest and KDP-safe (no reward offered for reviews). Good.
- Move the Part Three notes so they come before this page.

**Repeated phrases and patterns:**
- "honest/honestly" (30 times), "real" (68), "plain" (40), "simple" (33), "experience" (35). Each one is fine; together they become a slogan. Cut about half.
- Bold one-line morals at the end of nearly every section ("**Why it matters:**", "**The principle:**", "**Build something…**").
- "Not because X, but because Y" and "It was not X; it was Y" sentence shapes.
- Very many sentences follow the same three-part rhythm: "X, Y and Z".

**What to cut or shorten (estimated savings):**
- Chapters 2 + 3 → one chapter: save about 900–1,100 words.
- Chapter 4 → a short map: save about 500 words.
- Chapter 1 (Grantham/bubble section and cuts): save about 400 words.
- Chapter 9 (marketplaces and robots paragraph, some "why I tried"): save about 250 words.
- Chapter 12 (Hopf section, repeated safety points): save about 350 words.
- Repeated stories in Chapter 6: save about 300 words.

**Is the length right?** Yes, 20,000–25,000 words is a good length for this kind of Kindle book. Do not aim to make it longer. Cut about 3,000 words of off-topic and repeated material, then **add back** about 1,500–2,500 words of what readers want: setup help, screenshots with captions, real results and more of your own mistakes. The final length can stay about the same while the value rises a lot.

29. The book promotes one AI vendor heavily while quoting a researcher who criticises it. That is fair and balanced. Just make sure the Coxon part is accurate.

## 8. The Amazon description

**What works**
- The opening line is concrete and specific ("four real business tools… about $31 a month").
- "Most books about AI promise easy money" sets up the contrast well.
- The bullet lists are easy to scan and accurate to the book.
- "No hype. No income promises." is a good closing line, and the "It is." ending has energy.
- About 230 words is a good length.

**What to improve**
1. **Lead with the reader, not the author.** Readers buy for their own problem. Consider opening with a question: "Have you heard that AI can build apps for you, but you have never written a line of code?" Then add your claim.
2. **Use your strongest differentiator: honest results.** Say it directly: "including a blog that has not made a single sale yet, and exactly why." This is surprising and builds trust.
3. **Fix the "four tools" claim.** Call Project Four "a book-writing workflow" (issue 8).
4. **Reframe "computer science graduate".** For a "No Coding" book this raises a doubt. Use "a market researcher and delivery-business owner who had never built an app".
5. **Mention the practical extras**: copy-and-paste prompts, "Builder's Notes" in every project chapter, a glossary of plain-English terms.
6. **Add search words naturally**: "Claude", "AI agent", "no-code", "vibe coding", "small business automation". (KDP allows brand names in the description when used descriptively.)
7. **Do not paste the editor notes**: remove the heading "## 2. Amazon book description (approved draft)" and the line "KDP supports simple formatting…" before pasting into KDP. The keywords section in the file is empty. Fill all seven keyword fields.
8. Avoid anything time-sensitive (e.g. the launch offer) in the description.

**Suggested revised description (about 220 words)**

> **Never written a line of code? Neither had I. Then I built three real business tools with AI, for about $31 a month.**
>
> Most books about AI promise easy money. This one shows what really happens when a small-business owner in Cambodia sits down with an AI agent and starts building: the decisions, the costs, the mistakes, and the results so far, including a blog that has not made a single sale yet, and why.
>
> **Follow real projects, step by step:**
> - **A Telegram booking app** for a delivery business that costs nothing to run
> - **A blog that drafts its own articles**, with every post checked by a person, and an honest look at "passive income"
> - **A video ad factory** that makes short ads in Khmer and English without a video editor
> - **A book-writing workflow** that helped plan, research and write this book
>
> **Inside, you will learn:**
> - How to talk to AI so it builds what you actually need
> - How to build your first small tool in about 30 minutes
> - What building with AI really costs, in money and in time
> - How to protect your passwords, your customers' data and your reputation
> - A simple seven-day plan to start your own first project
>
> Each project chapter ends with **Builder's Notes**: copy-and-paste prompts, setup steps and common traps.
>
> **No hype. No income promises. Just real tools, honestly built.**

## 9. If you only have one week: the 5 changes to make first

1. **Cut Part One down to size.** Merge Chapters 2 and 3 into one short chapter. Delete the war paragraph and the employer ethics accusation. Cut Chapter 4 to a one-page map. Building should start much earlier in the book. *(Issues 1, 5, 6)*
2. **Add the real numbers.** Fill every ⭐ (especially hours spent in Chapter 12), add booking-app usage to Chapter 8, put a dated "Status" box at the end of each project chapter, and rewrite the time-sensitive end of Chapter 11. *(Issue 2)*
3. **Add a "Getting Set Up" section with screenshots.** Show how to open and use Claude Code, connect GitHub, and approve changes. Add 6–10 screenshots across the project chapters. *(Issues 3, 10)*
4. **Do a fact-and-rules check.** Verify the Coxon story and every 2026 news claim against original sources; replace weak links. Re-check Amazon Associates, GitHub Pages, Claude plan and KDP wording. Rename the "ebook machine" honestly in the book and the description. *(Issues 7, 8, 9)*
