# Is Your Home Ready? What Governments Now Recommend You Keep in an Emergency Kit

*October 2026*

In the past two years, a growing list of governments has asked people the same question: if the power, water or card payments stopped working tomorrow, could your household manage on its own for a few days?

Sweden mailed every household a booklet called *In case of crisis or war*. Norway raised its advice from three days of supplies to a full week. The European Union asked all citizens to be able to manage for at least 72 hours. The UK launched public advice on preparing for emergencies, and news outlets report that Japan is preparing expanded guidance too.

Headlines often link this to fears of war. The official advice is calmer and more practical than that. The same kit covers a winter storm, a flood, a long power cut, a cyberattack on a payment system or a broken water main. You don't need a bunker. You need a few boxes of the right things, kept in one place, and a plan your family has talked through once.

This guide sums up what the main national guidelines recommend and turns it into one checklist you can build over a few weeks.

---

## What different countries recommend

| Country or body | How long to be self-sufficient | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **European Union** | At least 72 hours | Part of the EU's 2025 Preparedness Union Strategy |
| **Finland, France, Lithuania, Czechia, Ireland** | About 72 hours (3 days) | Finland's long-running "72 hours" campaign is the model many others follow |
| **United Kingdom** | A few days (reports cite 3) | Food, water, a torch, a battery radio, cash and medicines |
| **United States** | "Several days" | Ready.gov / FEMA kit list; 1 gallon of water per person per day |
| **Sweden** | About 1 week | Includes warm clothing, a backup heat source and cash in case digital payments fail |
| **Norway** | 1 week | Raised from 72 hours in 2025 |
| **Germany** | Up to 10 days | Long-standing advice from the federal civil protection office (BBK) |
| **Japan** | 3 days minimum, ideally 1 week | Expanded guidance reported in 2026 |

**The simple rule:** aim for **3 days first**, then build toward **1 week**. Three days covers most short emergencies; a week covers the longer outages northern Europe now plans for.

## The checklist

Amounts below are per person unless they say otherwise. Multiply by the people (and pets) in your home.

### 1. Water: the most important item
- **Drinking water:** about **4 liters (1 gallon) per person per day**, which covers drinking and a little cooking and washing. For 3 days, that's 12 liters each; for a week, about 28 liters each.
- **A way to treat more water:** water purification tablets or a simple filter, in case your supply runs out.
- **Extra water for washing and flushing:** fill a bathtub or large containers when a storm or outage is announced.
- Store bottled water somewhere cool and dark, and replace it by the date on the label.

### 2. Food that needs no fridge
- **Ready-to-eat food:** canned fish, beans, meat and vegetables; peanut butter; crackers; nuts; dried fruit; cereal bars; shelf-stable milk.
- **Easy-to-cook staples**, if you'll have a way to heat water: rice, pasta, oats, instant noodles, soup mixes.
- **A manual can opener.** An electric one is useless in a power cut.
- **Comfort food:** chocolate, tea, coffee, snacks the children like. Small comforts matter in a stressful week.
- **Special diets:** baby food and formula, food for allergies, and **pet food**.

**Tip:** buy what you already eat. Put new items at the back and use the oldest first, so nothing goes to waste and your stock stays fresh without extra effort.

### 3. Light, power and information
- **Flashlights or headlamps** for each person, plus **spare batteries**. Avoid relying on candles; they're a fire risk.
- **A battery-powered or hand-crank radio**, so you can hear official updates if phones and the internet go down. Several governments list this specifically.
- **Power banks**, kept charged, for your phones.
- **A paper list of important phone numbers** (family, doctor, neighbors, insurance), because your phone may be dead.

### 4. Health and first aid
- **A first aid kit:** bandages, gauze, tape, antiseptic, scissors, tweezers, gloves, a thermometer.
- **Prescription medicines** for at least a week, plus copies of prescriptions. Ask your doctor or pharmacist how to keep a small extra supply safely.
- **Everyday medicines:** pain and fever relief, allergy tablets, stomach remedies, oral rehydration salts.
- **Glasses or contact lenses**, and supplies for any medical devices.
- **Face masks**, which help with dust, smoke or illness.

### 5. Hygiene and sanitation
- Toilet paper, wet wipes, hand sanitizer, soap, toothbrushes and toothpaste, menstrual products and diapers.
- **Heavy-duty garbage bags and ties**, which double as an emergency toilet liner if water is cut off.
- Household bleach (unscented) for cleaning, kept away from food and children.

### 6. Warmth and shelter
- **Warm clothes, hats and gloves, and blankets or sleeping bags** for everyone. Nordic guidance stresses this for winter power cuts.
- **Sturdy shoes** and rain gear.
- Plastic sheeting and duct tape to cover a broken window.

### 7. Money and documents
- **Cash in small notes.** Card machines and ATMs stop working without power or network. Sweden and Norway specifically advise keeping some cash because digital payments can fail.
- **Copies of important documents** (ID, passports, insurance, property papers, medical information) in a waterproof bag, plus a copy stored securely online.
- **Spare keys** for home and car.

### 8. Cooking without power (optional, with care)
- A camping stove with fuel, or a charcoal grill, lets you heat food and boil water.
- **Safety:** never use a camping stove, grill, generator or charcoal indoors or in a garage. They produce carbon monoxide, which can kill without warning. Use them outside only, away from windows, and fit a carbon monoxide alarm at home.

### 9. Tools and small extras
- A multi-tool or basic tool kit, matches or a lighter in a waterproof container, and a whistle to signal for help.
- A **fire extinguisher** and working smoke alarms.
- A printed map of your area.
- Games, books or cards for children. Long outages are boring as well as stressful.

## A grab bag for leaving in a hurry

If you might need to leave home quickly (a flood, a fire or an evacuation order), keep a smaller **grab bag** by the door:

- Water and snacks for 1 day
- Flashlight, power bank and charging cable
- First aid kit and medicines
- Cash and copies of documents
- A change of clothes, a warm layer and rain gear
- Phone numbers on paper
- Hygiene basics and face masks

---

## Make a plan, not just a pile

Supplies help most when your family knows what to do:

1. **Agree on a meeting place** near home, and one further away, in case you're separated.
2. **Choose one contact person outside your area** whom everyone calls or messages to say they're safe.
3. **Know how to turn off** your water, gas and electricity.
4. **Know your local warning system**: sirens, emergency text alerts and the official radio station.
5. **Check on neighbors**, especially older people living alone. Community help is a big part of every country's advice.

---

## Building it without panic or overspending

- **Spread it out.** Add a few items to each weekly shop instead of buying everything at once.
- **Don't panic-buy.** Emptying shelves before an emergency makes things harder for everyone, including you.
- **Start with what you have.** Many homes already own half of this list; gather it into one box so you can find it in the dark.
- **Set a reminder twice a year** (many people use the clock changes) to check dates, replace batteries and update medicines.

---

## Why governments are asking now

Officials point to several overlapping risks: more frequent extreme weather, cyberattacks on power grids and payment systems, sabotage of undersea cables and energy links, and a tense security situation in Europe. Many European countries are also reopening civil-defense shelters and increasing defense spending.

None of this means a crisis is about to happen. It means governments want households to be able to look after themselves for a few days, so emergency services can focus on the people who need them most. A ready household isn't afraid; it's simply less likely to be caught out.
